# Chapter 79
## Pyramids And Capstones

Joshua brought a children’s book about Egypt to the café because he had been babysitting and because the universe has a sense of humour about visual aids.

‘Look,’ he said, spinning the picture toward Michael and R. ‘Everyone loves a pyramid. Clean edges. Clear top. The fantasy that someone finished the shape and the desert applauded.’

Michael studied the illustration: tidy sand, heroic scale, no slave dust, no modern politics, a sun that looked like a sticker. ‘Capstone on top. The little pyramid that completes the big one. Museums love that bit. Men who want to be inevitable love that bit more.’

R. sipped tea, eyes on the drawing. ‘And every tradition has people who think they are the capstone. Final prophet. Final council. Final science. Final joke about fonts. Final man who “just noticed.”’

He accepted the hit with a nod that cost something. ‘So what’s the useful version of the image? Because my brain likes pyramids. Hierarchy. Ascent. The idea that if I climb enough noticing I reach the tip and see the plan laid out like a model village.’

Joshua tapped the page, leaving a small crescent of croissant grease that made the Nile look expensive. ‘Useful version: lots of human labour. Base wider than ego. Capstone as temptation. Also: most of the stone is not the tip. If you only love the tip, you’re in love with prestige, not with building. Prestige is a thin god. It starves in winter.’

A child at another table shouted about dinosaurs, which felt thematically adjacent—big shapes, extinction, museums. The café machine screamed milk into froth. Civilisation continued its small industrial noises.

Michael tore a corner from his receipt and sketched a pyramid, then a little cap floating above it with a gap. ‘What if the gap is the point? What if completion is a claim that should stay slightly unseated so no one uses it as a throne? Not as an insult to people who need completion language for integrity—R., I’m looking near you, not at you—but as a restraining order on me.’

R. considered, turning the children’s book so the Sphinx stopped staring at her scone. ‘In my language, seal is not your floating cap for doodling. Don’t. But I understand the warning about tips. Power loves tops. Tops love pretending they are inevitable. Your gap is a personal ethic. Keep it personal. Don’t baptise my seal into your gap.’

‘Heard,’ Michael said. ‘Empire again in geometry form.’

‘Geometry of empire,’ Joshua agreed. ‘Pyramids look like forever. They’re weather plus politics plus time. Sand takes them down an inch at a time. So does truth-telling. So does a friend who says *get off the peak*.’

Michael thought of the monogram—not a pyramid, a pair of letters, horizontal mischief rather than vertical climb. He said so, and R. smiled—not applause, permission to keep the joke from becoming a ranking system.

‘Good,’ she said. ‘Keep your symbol horizontal when it wants to become a ladder. The moment the joke becomes *I have the top*, you’ve installed a capstone on a laugh and killed it. Dead jokes make dangerous religions.’

Joshua closed the children’s book. Cleopatra’s eye on the cover stared like a tired librarian who had seen too many projects.

‘Homework,’ Joshua said. ‘Today, notice one pyramid you climb for status—work, intellect, purity, suffering, “most awake person in the group chat”—and step one block down on purpose. Be base. Be load-bearing. Be boring stone. Text us when you’ve been boring on purpose.’

Michael groaned. ‘You’re vicious when you babysit.’

‘Children teach scale,’ Joshua said. ‘Also they drop books in puddles. Humility curriculum. The book survived. Barely. Like most of us.’

That afternoon Michael answered an email without proving he was the smartest person in the thread. He let Cameron’s ordinary answer stand in a chat without a both/and flourish. He washed the base of things: plates, socks, the bit of the day that never gets a documentary. He texted the group: *Was boring. Survived. Peak can wait.*

R.: *Proud of your stone.*

Cameron: *Forty-two is a base number. Stay there.*

Joshua: *Send photo of clean sink if you need accountability theatre. Optional. Stone doesn’t need selfies.*

Evening, on the phone to R., he said, ‘I didn’t solve the capstone problem.’

‘You’re not supposed to solve it in one café,’ she said. ‘You’re supposed to stop applying for the job of peak. Applications denied. Next. And don’t write a chapter where you secretly crown yourself Most Humble Base. That’s peak with a costume.’

‘Caught,’ he said.

‘Good. Catch yourself faster next time.’

Harriet found him later stacking the dishwasher with the concentration of a man performing penance.

‘You’re being suspiciously ordinary,’ she said.

‘Joshua’s homework,’ Michael said. ‘I’m practising being base.’

‘You’ve always been base,’ she said. ‘You just forget and audition for apex predator of the group chat. Keep stacking. Stacking is honest. Peaks are for people who haven’t done the washing-up.’

He stacked. A plate clicked into place like a small agreement with gravity. Somewhere in a museum a model pyramid waited for tomorrow’s school group. Somewhere in a wallet a napkin waited without needing to be the tip of history.

He slept without crowning anything. The gap above the stones, for one night, felt less like failure than like air that everyone could still breathe.
