# Chapter 58
## Not Either/Or

The fight arrived exactly on schedule.

Not with Joshua—with a version of Michael that still loved fences, and with a message board in his head where strangers were already typing.

*It’s just a dick joke.*  
*It’s pure mysticism ruined.*  
*Pick one: Revelation or toilet wall.*

Michael sat on the bed with the napkin on his knee and practised refusing the fork. The biro lines looked almost patient. Paper has that gift: it does not hurry your cowardice.

“Not either/or,” he said aloud. The room accepted the sound without charging rent.

Joshua was on the phone buying milk for his own house—plastic bag rustle, shop radio, life continuing its logistics. “Go on,” he said.

“If I only keep the pious span,” Michael said, “I protect respectability and lose the generative honesty the closed map requires. If I only keep the rude glyph, I get a viral shock and lose the claim that made the glyph *matter*. Either/or is the blind guides’ favourite tool. It sorts the world into teams and sells tickets.”

“And both/and?” Joshua asked, though he knew.

“Both/and is not mush,” Michael said. “It’s load-bearing here. The monogram *is* first-and-last language for the whole. The monogram *also* rhymes, in living fonts, with both generative altars. The light in the reader’s mind chooses which office opens first. The letters don’t have to divorce to keep custody of meaning.”

A till beeped in Joshua’s background. He paid, returned. “Cameron will say polysemy. Juxtaposition. Definition game.”

“He’s right in classical court,” Michael said. “It *is* polysemy. It *is* juxtaposition. If you only count one temple at a time, you can always claim the other is projection. The novel’s job isn’t to win a formal logic trophy against him. It’s to make the recognition undeniable as *recognition*—the laugh that happens when the chain has already removed the elsewhere.”

“Say that again slower for the cheap seats.”

Michael smiled at the empty half of the bed. “We didn’t start with genitals. We started with experience that won’t unplug, nothing that can’t be a real outside, hell-as-exit failing, sin-as-landfill failing, poles that generate, a total without a locker room. *Then* the drawing. The drawing is a consequence, not a stunt.”

He messaged Cameron the spine of it, short enough to survive sarcasm:

*Not either/or. Pious span and dual body-rhyme. Your court still valid for single-temple counting. Novel = long recognition engine.*

Cameron: *I heard my name. Yes: polysemy. Yes: you can’t force a formal identity between ink and ontology without a frame. Also yes: as a human who has eyes, I see what you see. I can grant the funny without crowning the bailey.*

Michael: *That’s all I wanted. The grant. Not the crown.*

Cameron: *Then we remain friends. Miraculous.*

Joshua came round later with the milk still cold in a bag and the look of a man who had decided the kitchen table was safer than the phone. They put both readings on paper in two columns, then drew arrows both ways until the columns looked silly—which was the point. Span claim pointing at body rhyme. Body rhyme pointing at span. First-and-last holding hands with enter-and-receive. Whole covered; both altars. No generative half exiled as “merely crude.”

Joshua tapped the middle of the page where the arrows crossed. “Either/or wants a winner.”

“Not either/or wants a marriage.”

“Some marriages are jokes,” Michael said.

“The best ones are,” Joshua said. “They still do the dishes.”

Michael folded the double-column sheet and put it under the napkin like a second treaty. The biro monogram sat on top, unbothered by their bureaucracy. He felt the old team-sport brain try one more draft of a speech for imaginary critics—the kind of speech that wins applause and loses the field. He left it unsaid. Joshua poured the milk into Michael’s fridge as if that, too, were part of the claim: ordinary continuity, both hands.

Evening, Michael walked a street that held a church and a pub in the same block—an old arrangement cities invent without reading his book. Bells and beer, stone and sticky carpet. He did not need them to merge into one building. He needed to stop believing only one of them owned truth’s lease. A man outside the pub laughed at a joke Michael could not hear. Inside the church porch, a noticeboard offered a jumble sale with the quiet confidence of continuity.

At the desk he practised the chapter’s spine not as a slogan for a poster but as a sentence he could say to a stranger without becoming a salesman: the light chooses; the field contains.

Then, because practice matters more than mottoes, he answered a work email with ordinary competence and washed a plate. The monogram watched from its pin. It did not demand incense. It demanded continuity—the same continuity that had carried him from cold floors to napkins without requiring a pure elsewhere to store the awkward bits.

Before sleep he checked the temptation to recruit strangers into agreement. He let it pass like a bus he did not need. Recognition is not a press gang. The book could set a table; it could not force anyone to sit. That limit was not a failure of the claim. It was the claim remaining kind.

The napkin’s biro lines stayed double: equal, equal.

Two strokes.

One claim that refused to pick a team.
