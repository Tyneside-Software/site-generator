# Chapter 25
## Different Speeds

Morning after the long night arrived like a bus that had not read the crisis.

Sunlight did not apologise for being ordinary. The kettle did not ask about abyss. Michael's body, traitor and friend, wanted toast more than ontology, which was either a failure of seriousness or the first sign of health.

His eyes felt scraped. The note on his phone still held both weathers—terror of layers, and the smaller honest line that he was enjoying the journey anyway. Sisyphus had been on holiday for a few dark hours and then, apparently, clocked back in as breakfast.

He rang Joshua.

'Survived,' Michael said. 'Different speeds.'

'Amen. Drink water. Call Cameron if you can bear ordinary genius.'

Cameron was the friend who treated the universe like a hitchhiker's problem with a known numeric punchline. Michael had been circling him all week without landing—mentioning him to Joshua like a weather front still offshore. Today the front came inland: café table, two coffees, Cameron's calm face.

'You look like you've been negotiating with God,' Cameron said.

'With layers. God may have been the weather.'

Cameron stirred sugar he did not need. 'I'm still at forty-two, mate. Adams had it right. The answer is ordinary. The mistake is building cathedrals out of the question.'

Michael almost flinched, then recognised the gift. After a night of infinite hatches, ordinary was not an insult. It was a seatbelt.

'I'm going faster,' Michael said—not as boast, as report. 'Not better. Faster. Joshua keeps saying amen to the direction and still tells me to wash up.'

'Joshua would. I'm the brakes. You're the engine. Someone has to be the speed limit painted on the road.'

They sat with that. Around them people moved at every velocity of soul: a student highlighting a textbook as if the exam were death; a pensioner reading a paper with luxurious slowness; a toddler discovering crumbs at the pace of pure research.

'Everyone's on the same journey,' Michael said, hearing his own night-thought out loud. 'Different speeds.'

Cameron nodded. 'Some of us stop at the scenic overlook labelled forty-two and have a sandwich. Some of you try to outrun death by understanding. I don't hate you for it. I do reserve the right to go to bed.'

Michael laughed, raw and grateful. 'I need that. The night tried to convince me that only the deepest layer counted. Then the cat outside the window counted more than the abyss for ten whole seconds.'

'Cats are undefeated. Listen—I'm not going to race you. If you bring me a symbol and crown it The Answer, I'll throw a pillow at you. If you bring me a joke that helps you be kinder, I'll laugh. Deal?'

'Deal.'

Joshua joined them twenty minutes later, as if summoned by the word *amen* still lingering in the air. He looked at Michael's face, then at Cameron's, and smiled the small smile of a man who liked his tools arranged.

'Different speeds,' Joshua said.

'Already covered,' Cameron said.

'Amen,' Joshua replied, and made it sound like agreement with physics.

They talked about nothing heroic: a leaky tap, a funny email, whether the café's muffins were ambitious or merely large. Michael felt the layers recede to a manageable distance—still real, no longer the only weather. Cameron checked the time once, not rudely, just as a person who believed evenings existed for sleep.

'You're allowed to be ahead of me,' Cameron said when Michael started apologising for intensity. 'You're not allowed to call me unfinished for sitting at forty-two. Ordinary isn't a failure mode.'

Michael held the sentence like a warm mug. 'I needed sandpaper. Not to be smooth. To be shaped.'

'That's the nicest thing anyone's said about my personality. Don't make it weird.'

Walking home, Michael matched his pace to an old man with a shopping trolley and refused the urge to overtake on principle. Speed was not virtue. Direction might be. Kindness almost certainly was. The old man stopped to speak to a dog; the dog considered the treaty and accepted a brief ear-scratch. Two lives, two tempos, one pavement. The night's terror loosened another notch—not gone, paced.

He thought of people he had judged as asleep at the wheel of existence. Some had been tired. Some had been wise. Some had been protecting children from a parent's hunger for significance. The long night had made him hungry for meaning in a way that could turn cruel if unsupervised. Cameron's forty-two was supervision with jokes.

At home he reread the despair paragraph once, without drowning, and the enjoyment line once, without forcing cheer. Both true. Different weather systems, one sky. Different speeds, one road.

He washed a plate. He opened a window. Somewhere a neighbour's radio played something cheerful and slightly wrong for the hour, which was perfect. The crisis had not been solved. It had been paced.

That was a different kind of answer—one that did not need a number, and still got him home.
