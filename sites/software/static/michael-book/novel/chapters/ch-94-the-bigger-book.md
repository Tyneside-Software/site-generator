# Chapter 94
## The Bigger Book

They met to name the thing properly—not as argument, as title.

Café, window table, rain performing its old play against glass that needed cleaning. R. with her notebook open to a blank page on purpose, which is a kind of courage. Michael with empty hands. Joshua present as midwife, already stirring sugar he did not need. Cameron present as the man who would say *forty-two* if the poetry swelled, unwrapping a sugar packet like a fuse he was prepared to light for everyone’s safety.

‘I wanted a bigger book,’ Michael said. ‘After the monogram. After the last line. I thought bigger meant more pages of me being right in new directions. It’s come to mean more room for people who aren’t my directions. Hospitality, not annexation. If that’s still ego in a nicer coat, tell me now—while the coffee’s hot and I can still blush.’

R. drew an open rectangle, not a circle. Pen sure. ‘Bigger book as hospitality. Not as sequel that eats prior volumes. Circles close. Rectangles leave a side for someone to enter. I’m fond of rectangles today. Circles make me claustrophobic when men draw them.’

Joshua smiled into his cup. ‘Canon as library, not as single scroll with your monogram as binding. Libraries let you leave with different books. Scrolls want to be the only meal and then wonder why guests look thin.’

Cameron stabbed the sugar packet once, gently. ‘Define success so we can audit you. Vague success is how cults file taxes. Also how clever men never fail because nothing was measurable.’

Michael thought of Denise’s jam, Harriet’s plates, soft-play knees, red lines said out loud in a park, NRE cooling into bin night, seal and page distinguished so they stopped bruising each other, a colleague’s boat with a hole, a keyboard left unbaptised, a canal walk that had refused to become a crusade.

‘Success,’ he said, counting on fingers where Cameron could see the digits and the honesty: ‘I keep learning without treating friends as chapters I control. I hold Christ == God without making R. a failed equation. I hold no-outside without erasing different boats. I keep writing—living—without demanding a publisher called Destiny. I repair when I break a word. I go home and wash up. If I can’t wash up, I’m not enlightened—I’m just busy being important.’

R. added, pen moving: ‘Success on my side: I remain unannexed and still invited. I correct you without becoming your conscience-for-hire. I keep my seal without needing you to hate your open page. I eat with you after hard sentences. Eating is non-negotiable. People who only debate starve the friendship.’

They sat with the mutual contract. The barista called a name that wasn’t theirs; someone else’s story collected a latte and left a tip that was either generous or guilty. Rain thickened. The window became a soft wall, and for a moment the café felt like a boat that had agreed not to sink for an hour.

Joshua raised his cup. ‘The bigger book is already being written. It’s the week. It’s the washing-up. It’s Scripture Reasoning without a winner. It’s Cameron leaving early. It’s Harriet at half six with plates. It’s Denise’s boiler. You don’t finish it. You inhabit it. Finishing is the capstone urge again—peak fever with better vocabulary.’

Michael felt the old hunger ease into something like vocation without badge. ‘Final chapter and bigger book—different objects. We said it over pasta. I feel it now in my bones. Her finality doesn’t close my homework. My homework doesn’t reopen her seal as if it were a zip file waiting for my password.’

‘Zip file,’ Cameron said. ‘Please never say that in a sermon. Or a café. Or near me. Or in a group chat where I have to see it twice.’

‘I’m not giving sermons,’ Michael said. ‘I’m buying the next round of tea. That is my missionary method for the next ten minutes. Limited scope. High calorie.’

He did. When he returned, careful with hot cardboard, R. had written on a napkin—danger stationery reclaimed from its history of monograms—**BIGGER BOOK = MORE ROOM, NOT MORE THRONE.**

Michael pocketed the napkin beside the monogram one. Two napkins. No merger. Wallet getting thick with ethics, thin with cash—as it should be, if ethics are costing something real.

Outside, under shared umbrellas that were not metaphors unless you were weak and lonely for symbolism, they parted at the corner where the bus threw water at their shoes with municipal indifference. R. said, ‘Title accepted. Live it. Don’t brand it. Branding is how hospitality becomes a logo.’ Joshua said amen, quiet enough not to make a scene. Cameron said forty-two and meant *don’t get weird in the group chat*, which is a blessing in modern English.

Michael walked the long way, past the canal, and did not open the notebook. He let the city write a few lines: bus hiss, wet leaves plastered to pavement like abandoned notes, a child in yellow wellies stamping a puddle into empire and then laughing the empire away when a parent called time. The bigger book had no last page he could own, no ISBN that made him the author of other people’s endings.

At the bridge he stopped. Water moved under him, carrying rain that had once been sky. He thought of the napkin pair in his pocket—recognition and hospitality, joke and room—and of how a man can hold both without crowning either.

He went home. Harriet had left a plate of something under foil and a note that said *eat, then talk if you must*. He ate. He washed the plate. He did not brand the washing-up as a spiritual practice out loud, though privately he knew Joshua would have called it sacred plumbing and meant it.

The bigger book turned another damp page without asking him to crown it. The funny old life, which had always been larger than one climax, kept raining, kept inviting, kept refusing to finish other people for the convenience of a neat spine.
