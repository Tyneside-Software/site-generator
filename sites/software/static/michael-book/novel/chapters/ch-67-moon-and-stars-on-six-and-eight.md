# Chapter 67
## Moon And Stars On Six And Eight

By mid-morning the song had faded and the hardware had not.

Michael sat at the desk with the intention of doing email, which is how many revelations get past security. His hands found the home row the way hands do when they have typed a life. Then they stopped.

Keys six and eight.

He had lived with them for years without ceremony. Six next to five and seven; eight next to seven and nine; ordinary neighbours in a plastic suburb. During the systems week he and Joshua had made jokes about six and seven—about fences, about not crowning punctuation. Eight had waited like a spare punchline in the drawer, polite enough not to interrupt.

Now he looked properly.

On this keyboard the number keys carried secondary symbols, the kind you only notice when you need them for a password or a programming tantrum. The legends were worn, half-legible, personal as fingerprints. He tilted the board toward the window light and his mind, still high from the napkin, did what high minds do: it started building bridges out of scuffs.

A curve that might be a crescent if you squinted with hope. An asterisk on eight sitting above like a cheap star. Not a design conspiracy. Not a manufacturer’s secret theology. Just plastic, abrasion, light, and a brain that had spent a night arguing with God about fonts and now wanted the furniture to join the conversation.

‘No,’ he said aloud. ‘We are not doing fridge magnets.’

His phone buzzed. Cameron: *You alive after the monogram pub?*

Michael: *Alive. Currently trying not to baptise my keyboard into comparative religion.*

Cameron: *Good. Keyboards are for work. Work is forty-two with extra steps. Don’t start a cult with Shift.*

Michael almost put the board face-down, like a suspect. Then he did something better: he photographed nothing. He wrote nothing mystical on social media. He made fresh tea with the concentration of a man defusing himself, and rang Joshua.

‘I’m looking at six and eight,’ Michael said without preamble.

‘Congratulations. Most people manage that by year three of childhood.’

‘The moon-and-star itch. After the song. After the napkin. My brain wants a chain link. I can feel it building a cathedral out of worn legends and private cleverness.’

Joshua was quiet long enough to be useful. In the background a kettle boiled; ordinary life providing the soundtrack of sanity.

‘What’s the red line?’ Joshua asked.

‘R.,’ Michael said. ‘And the bookshop vow. Capture is not curiosity. Other people’s sacred signs are not my alphabet to rearrange for fun.’

‘Then keep the itch as data about *you*,’ Joshua said. ‘Not as a decoding of Islam. Your mind is high after α==ω. High minds invent bridges. Some bridges are real. Some are ego with scaffolding and a hard hat that says *insight*.’

Michael pressed six, then eight, gently, as if apologising to them for the attention. Click. Click. No angel appeared. The cursor jumped in a document titled `TODO`, which was, he had to admit, a more honest oracle than most.

‘I want the bigger book,’ he admitted. ‘I also want not to be the man who turns his friend’s faith into a keyboard Easter egg and calls it reverence.’

‘Both,’ Joshua said. ‘Hold both. Walk to the shop. Buy milk. If the moon and star still matter this afternoon, they can matter as *people*, not as keys. People answer back. Keys only pretend to.’

Michael stood. The board stayed on the desk, demoted from oracle to tool, which is where tools are happiest. He put on a coat that smelled faintly of last night’s pub and locked the door on the temptation to stay and keep staring until the plastic confessed.

In the stairwell he thought of R.—not as a symbol, not as a proof that his system already included the world, but as a person who would correct him if he got clever in the wrong direction. The thought was a relief. Correction from a friend is cheaper than the bill that arrives when cleverness has already spent the friendship.

Outside, the day did not wear a crescent. It wore traffic, a man swearing at a ticket machine, and a woman walking a dog that refused to negotiate with a lamppost. The corner shop sold milk that was almost in date and a newspaper that believed everything was urgent. Michael paid. The till drawer opened with a chime that meant nothing cosmic and everything practical.

On the way back he glanced once at his own hands on the carrier bag. Six and eight had typed thousands of emails, passwords, apologies, shopping lists. They had earned the right to be numbers again.

He climbed the stairs. Inside, the keyboard waited, innocent, slightly dirty, entirely willing to return to work if he would only stop auditioning it for scripture.

Michael set the milk in the fridge, sat down, and opened email. Subject lines about invoices and meetings and a calendar invite that assumed he was a professional. He typed. Six and eight did their jobs without commentary.

That, too, was a kind of mercy—the day wearing traffic instead of a symbol, the keys staying keys, the itch remaining data about a man who had learned something true and must not harvest other people’s moons to prove it.

By afternoon the itch had thinned to a memory of an itch. He texted Joshua a photograph of the milk on the counter, captioned only: *Bought dairy. Keyboard remains unbaptised.*

Joshua: *Hallelujah. Now do the washing-up.*

Michael did the washing-up. The day, unimpressed and kind, continued.
