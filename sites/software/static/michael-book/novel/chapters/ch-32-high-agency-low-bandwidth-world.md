# Chapter 32
## High Agency, Low Bandwidth World

Evening. Home. The kind of domestic quiet that looks like peace from the street and feels like a negotiation from the sofa.

Michael had been talking—carefully at first, then with the heat that always arrived when the numbers got large. Distant deaths. Preventable misery. The way the news turned horror into weather if it happened far enough away. He could feel his own voice climbing the ladder of urgency while the room stayed stubbornly local: laundry basket, half-done homework on the table, the television offering a programme about houses someone else could afford.

She was tired. Not wicked. Not shallow. Tired in the way bodies get tired after a day that had already spent its care budget on the people in front of them.

'I can't do this tonight,' she said. 'I need to sleep.'

The irony landed in Michael's chest like a coin he had flipped a thousand times. He had just said that millions die because people protect their comfort. She had just protected her sleep. The universe, which has a sense of humour with excellent timing, did not even bother to underline it.

He said, 'Right,' because *right* is what high-agency people say when they are trying not to turn love into a tribunal.

She went upstairs. A door closed with the soft finality of bandwidth hitting zero.

Michael sat in the glow of the muted television and felt the old isolation rise—the sense of being a furnace in a street of carefully banked fires.

He rang Joshua. Road-noise answered first, then kindness.

'How do you live,' Michael said, 'as a high-agency person who can't unsee the scale, in a world where most people have low bandwidth and go to bed?'

'You don't live by requiring them to match your temperature. You live by treating their bandwidth as physics, not betrayal.'

'It feels like betrayal.'

'I know. Feeling isn't the same as a court case.' A pause. A car horn on Joshua's end. 'Proximity bias. Psychic numbing. Finite empathy. You've read the papers. Tonight you met the papers in a dressing gown.'

Michael walked to the kitchen and opened the fridge for no reason, which is what people do when ontology gets personal. 'My comfort feels insignificant next to the horror.'

'Your comfort is insignificant as a moral *goal*. Your capacity is not insignificant as an *instrument*. If you burn the instrument to prove you care, the horror keeps the win.'

Michael shut the fridge. 'That's the speech people give when they want permission to stay small.'

'No. That's the speech people give when they want you still standing in five years. High agency is not infinite agency. Low bandwidth around you is not a personal insult. It's the median climate. You build inside the climate. You don't wait for the climate to become you.'

Cameron joined the call without ceremony—Joshua must have patched him in, or the universe had.

'Also,' Cameron said, 'wanting sleep after work is not "not caring about distant deaths". It's not being a villain for having a nervous system.'

'I know. The ache is still real.'

'Ache can be real without being a mandate to judge the household.'

Joshua almost laughed. 'He's right again. Annoying habit.'

Michael leaned on the counter. 'I'm not satisfied to ignore the problems.'

'Then don't ignore them. Select. Leverage. Systems. The few people who can walk with you. Measure by what moves, not by how many bystanders *get it*. Emotional consensus from the tired majority is a terrible KPI.'

Michael looked up the stairs. Love lived up there, bandwidth-limited and real. Work lived in him, scale-haunted and real. The conflict was not a puzzle with one tidy piece missing. It was the ordinary tragedy of finite creatures who can model infinite need.

He said goodnight to both of them. He washed a plate that did not need washing. He stood in the doorway of the bedroom long enough to hear steady breathing, then retreated like a man who had remembered that presence can be quiet.

He almost reopened the conversation upstairs—one more paragraph about the millions, one more attempt to make the temperature match. He stopped with his hand on the banister. That would not have been courage. That would have been extracting agreement from a tired nervous system to soothe his own loneliness.

Outside, the town dimmed room by room.

Inside, Michael practised the hardest skill on the list: staying awake to the scale of things without turning the people he loved into evidence for the prosecution.

He did not sleep quickly. High agency rarely does. But when sleep came, it came without a speech, which was progress of a sort the horror statistics would never applaud and the household needed anyway.

Tomorrow he would work. Tomorrow the scale would still be there. Tonight the work was simpler and somehow harder: let low bandwidth be real without calling it evil, and let his own fire burn without demanding the whole street catch.
