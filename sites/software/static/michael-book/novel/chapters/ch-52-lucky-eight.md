# Chapter 52
## Lucky Eight

Eight had always been a friendly number.

Infinity stood up and put on a belt. A snowman. A pair of glasses. Lucky in some cultures, anxious in others, ordinary on a birthday cake with too much icing. Michael drew one on the fogged kitchen window with a finger and watched it weep down the glass into a sad little nothing, which felt on-brand for the week.

“Omega is eight hundred,” he told the glass. “You are not a silent theft. You are a cousin who visits.”

The window did not argue. Windows know about cousins.

Rain needled the back step. Joshua arrived stamping wet off his shoes, coat smelling of bus and weather, holding a bag of shopping as if groceries were a form of pastoral care.

“Talking to glass again?” Joshua asked.

“Lucky eight,” Michael said. “Half-veiled on purpose. I can feel the crude joke wanting the microphone—*eight equals*—” He stopped, laughed, shook his head. “Not yet. Bookstore rules. But the shape is in the room. Sideways loops. People have been drawing priapic cartoons since charcoal was invented. I’m not inventing dirt. I’m noticing a rhyme the chain might earn.”

Joshua set the bag down and filled the kettle, then paused with his hand still on the handle. “Earn how?”

“By not needing it,” Michael said. “If no-outside and no-sin-as-exile and genderless total and generative poles already hold, then a lucky visual is garnish. If I lean on the garnish as proof, Cameron wins by knockout.”

“Cameron isn’t here.”

“He’s always here. He’s the part of me that loves me enough to be rude.”

As if summoned by narrative law, the phone buzzed on the counter.

Cameron: *Ears burning. If you’re about to encode a willy as theology, remember I have screenshots of your sensible days.*

Michael typed with wet fingers: *Lucky eight only. Flagged as rhyme. ω stays 800. No punchline yet.*

Cameron: *I hate how careful you’re being. It makes my job harder.*

Michael: *Love you too.*

They took tea to the back step anyway, under the little roof that made rain sound important. A spider had built a web that looked like a flawed eight. Nature plagiarises without applying for grants.

“In music, octaves,” Joshua said. “Return with difference. In stories, eight as restart after seven’s completion. You don’t need the crude cousin for eight to be generative.”

“I know.” Michael blew on tea. “The crude cousin is still family. I’m not disinviting him from the wedding. I’m asking him not to do a speech before the vows.”

Joshua laughed into his mug. “There’s the book’s tone.”

Next door a child shouted *eight!* in a chalk game whose rules changed mid-shout. The number flew over the fence like a ball. Michael caught it mentally and tossed it back without annexing the child into a symbol system. That, too, was ethics.

“Does lucky feel true?” Joshua asked.

“Lucky feels like a mood the field sometimes wears,” Michael said. “Not a guarantee. I’ve had eights that were bus numbers to bad days. Still—when the chain lines up and an eight winks, I can accept a wink. Winks aren’t warrants. We said that about appointed hours.”

“Consistency,” Joshua said. “Annoying how often it helps.”

Inside, Michael cooked pasta like a man who understood that generative poles include boiling water and waiting. Salt. Steam. The soft violence of a rolling boil. He ate standing up, which was not a symbol, only hunger. Joshua stole a piece of penne from the colander with the innocence of brothers everywhere.

After Joshua left, Michael wiped the window clean. The eight vanished. The glass held rain-light and neighbour-noise and his own reflected face looking slightly less afraid of the next chapters.

He wrote one line for later-him, the one who would want to rush:

**Lucky eight may open a door. It is not the house.**

Then he washed the pan carefully, as if care itself were a kind of rhyme with luck—not payment, not bargain, just the small competence of staying in a body that still had dishes.

The crude cousin waited in the hall with the Greek letters, humming, surprisingly patient for a cousin of that reputation.

Michael turned off the kitchen light and left him there. Timing was still ethics. Embarrassment was still a form of wisdom. And the house—actual house, not metaphor—held enough heat for one honest evening without needing a punchline to justify the pasta.
