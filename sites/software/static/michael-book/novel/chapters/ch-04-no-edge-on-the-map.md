# Chapter 4
## No Edge On The Map

Monday evening smelled of garlic and the particular optimism of pasta that has not yet been overcooked.

Joshua cooked. Michael was allowed to stir, which is brother-code for *you may participate without sabotaging dinner*. The kitchen window had gone dark enough to turn the glass into a mirror with pretensions; beyond it, rain finally arrived and began its long argument with the pavement.

‘Right,’ Joshua said, tipping onions into the pan with a confidence Michael reserved for software and apologies. ‘You said the whole thing has no outside. So does it have an edge?’

Michael stirred. ‘If pure nothing can’t be a border, then the whole can’t have a rim made of not-existing. You can’t put a fence made of absence around everything. The fence would have to *be*.’

‘So no edge on the map of the whole. Not “very big map with a distant cliff.” No cliff. No “here be dragons of pure void.” Just more map—or the kind of map that doesn’t do edges because edges are a local hobby.’

Michael thought of every atlas he had loved as a child, decorative monsters in the uncharted bits. Those monsters had been ink. Ink was something. Even “unknown” was a label stuck onto the known practice of labelling.

The pasta water began to mutter.

Joshua pointed at the wall clock with a wooden spoon. ‘That thing has edges. Twelve and something after. Batteries. A smug face. Local time. Clocks are brilliant liars if you ask them about the whole show.’

‘They measure inside the room. They don’t date the room itself from the outside. There is no “before the universe” stamped by a clock hanging in pure nothing, because pure nothing can’t hold a nail.’

‘And yet people talk about beginnings as if someone could stand on the porch of non-being with a stopwatch and a clipboard.’

Michael laughed, short and real. ‘I’d like to see the job advert. *Must be comfortable with non-existence and Excel.*’

Joshua salted the water. ‘Beginnings of things—fine. Births, big bangs as physics stories, the day you started a company, the first time you noticed you were awake. Those are chapters. A beginning of the whole *from nothing* is a sentence that borrows its grammar from the thing it claims to explain.’

‘Endings the same,’ Michael said. ‘End of a day. End of a life. End of a pan of pasta if we don’t pay attention. Not end of is-ness into a clean rival called isn’t.’

The pasta went in with a soft rush. For a while they did the useful human work of not talking while food became food. Rain ticked the glass. The extractor fan hummed like a small, devout insect.

When plates were on the table and the sauce had been declared good enough, Joshua spun his fork thoughtfully.

‘People hate this. Not the pasta. The no-edge bit. They want a story with a cover and a back page. They want to know when the book started so they can feel clever about the dedication.’

‘I want that too,’ Michael admitted. ‘I’m not made of different clay. I just keep finding that every “before” I invent is already standing in the kitchen with a plate.’

Joshua nodded. ‘Then we stop asking the whole for a rim and start asking better local questions. When did *this* start? What changes? What holds? Where does the map fold for practical travel—not for escaping the paper.’

Michael twirled pasta and thought of maps again: tube maps that lie about distance to tell the truth about connection; weather maps that pretend the air has borders; the little map on his phone that insisted the world was a blue marble you could pinch.

‘Pinching the world,’ he said aloud. ‘That’s the fantasy. That you could get outside the picture and crop it.’

‘You can crop photos. You cannot crop being. You only ever crop *views*.’

They ate. It was ordinary and excellent—the sort of excellence that does not need a review. Sauce on a chin; napkin; the small comedy of two men who had once fought over the last roast potato and now shared garlic without drama. After a time Michael fetched the cheap notebook from the morning and, under **Start here**, set three short bricks in ink—no pure nothing as rival; no outside to the room; no edge on the whole made of absence—then closed the book before the list could start performing.

Joshua had read upside down, a talent of brothers and teachers. ‘You’re collecting bricks.’

‘Things that refuse to be negotiated away. If the week keeps going like this, I’ll have a wall. Walls are useful. You can hang pictures. You can lean when you’re tired.’

‘Or bash your head. Depending on the day.’

They washed up together. Horizontal beam, Joshua had called it earlier; Michael was starting to hear the phrase as more than a joke about chores. Side-by-side work. The kind of load that is shared because two people are in the same room and the room has no tradesman’s entrance for glory.

When Joshua left, the flat was quiet in the post-pasta way: steam on the window, clean plates, the clock still smugly ticking its local truth. Michael wiped the table and found a long thread of onion skin stuck to the wood. He laughed at himself for almost making it symbolic, then binned it like a man who still lived in a kitchen rather than a parable.

He stood in the hall and did not open any more doors. He already knew what they did.

He went to bed with the rain still talking to the street, and the street still answering from inside the only map there was. The last thing he noticed before sleep was the clock’s soft tick: edges everywhere for days and hours—and still no edge on the whole.
