# Chapter 12
## Moon, Star, And Rules About Pictures

Friday morning Michael nearly walked past the display without seeing it, which is how most of the important things in cities survive.

A bookshop window: new paperbacks, a novel with a lighthouse on the cover, a cookbook promising joy in thirty minutes, and—tucked at the edge—a small stand of comparative religion titles. One spine showed a crescent and star in clean gold foil. Another showed a cross. Another a wheel. The window dresser had arranged them like polite guests at a dinner where nobody wanted a fight.

Michael stopped. The glass threw his face back at him between the books, a ghost browser.

He knew just enough to know he did not know enough. That was not modesty as fashion. That was factual inventory. Islam was not a flavour of the week he had lived inside. It was a vast lived world with prayers and law and poetry and ordinary people buying milk—millions of kitchens, millions of arguments, millions of kindnesses that had never asked for his opinion.

Joshua’s text from the night before returned: *Tomorrow the world gets wider than our two beams. Be careful and kind.*

He went into the shop because standing on the pavement staring felt like the start of a different mistake. A bell above the door made a polite sound. Somewhere deeper in the shop a child was being negotiated away from a display of stickers. The comparative shelf smelled of paper and dust and the faint vanilla of glue. He read three back covers and put two books back because wanting to own a subject is not the same as being ready to host it. He bought one slender book of essays by people who actually knew what they were talking about, and he stood at the counter with the quiet embarrassment of a man purchasing humility in softcover form.

The bookseller did not comment. Bookseller grace is one of the underrated civilisations.

On the way to the park he passed a bus stop advert for a streaming series about ancient empires—helmets, sand, the usual confident font—and felt the old itch to connect everything to everything. History as binge content; symbols as thumbnails. He let the itch pass without scratching. That, too, was practice: not every spark is a lamp worth carrying into someone else’s house.

At lunch he met Joshua in the park again. The optimistic tree had gained a plastic bag in its branches, flapping like a surrender flag.

‘I almost did a diagram,’ Michael said. ‘Moon and star next to the cross next to the plus sign. Neat little row. Then I heard myself becoming a man who collects other people’s sacred things like fridge magnets.’

Joshua unwrapped a sandwich. ‘Good catch. Rules about pictures matter. Not because God is frightened of ink. Because people live under symbols, and if you drag those symbols into your private museum without love, you’re not studying—you’re shoplifting.’

‘I don’t want to shoplift. I also don’t want to pretend the week has only been a Christian geometry lesson with a philosophical porch. The high street has more than one kind of certainty. The museum claim—everything fits—can’t mean everything gets translated into *my* beams by force.’

‘Both/And with teeth. Unity without erasure. You can notice a crescent the way you notice a glove on a wall: it is here, it belongs to the field of what-is. You cannot decide it secretly means your story in a clever font. That’s just either/or wearing a smile.’

Michael turned the book in his hands. ‘There’s a discipline in not drawing certain things. I used to think that was only fear. Sometimes it is fear. Sometimes it’s respect with a backbone—refusing the cheap picture that makes strangers easier to own.’

A woman walked past with a pushchair; a man on a bench fed pigeons with the fatal generosity that creates avian empires. The park continued being many lives long.

‘So what’s the move?’ Joshua asked. ‘For today. Not for the whole library of civilisation.’

‘Careful humility. Read before I speak. Ask before I map. Leave empty fields in my own data where other people’s names go—not *Unknown* as a dismissal, but reserved seats I don’t fill with my own face.’

Joshua raised his coffee in a small salute. ‘That’ll do. That’s horizontal beam work across difference. Vertical remains the whole that holds all of us without being any of our mascots alone.’

Michael thought of the preacher and the sceptic, of how quickly certainty became a weapon, of how his own week could become a weapon if he got drunk on pattern-finding. Pattern-finding was a joy. Joy without reverence curdled.

‘I will not,’ he said slowly, ‘use anyone’s moon and star as a prop in my clever week. If they appear later, they appear as guests—with rules, with names, with the right to correct me.’

‘Write that down. Next to *do not let the cross stay merely clever*. Same family of vow.’

Michael did—not in the notebook, which was at home, but on the bookshop receipt, because vows should not always wait for ideal stationery. Three short lines about curiosity without capture, and empty fields as seats for voices not yet earned. He folded the receipt into the book and let the ink dry against essays that were not his to summarise on a park bench.

He walked back to work with the book under his arm and the plastic bag still flapping in the tree. The city did not applaud. Cities rarely do when you choose not to be an idiot.

That felt, in its quiet way, like progress: not a new symbol conquered—

—but a picture not taken, on purpose, out of something that might one day be called love if he kept practising.
