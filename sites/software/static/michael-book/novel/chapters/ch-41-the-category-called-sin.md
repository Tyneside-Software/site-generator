# Chapter 41
## The Category Called Sin

Morning arrived without asking whether ontology was ready for it.

Michael stood at the kitchen counter with the same plate he had washed the night before and the same map that no longer had a beyond. Sleep had not reinstalled an outside. The crisis had only changed clothes: less panic, more paperwork.

If there was no true cut in the field of being, what happened to the old moral landfill?

He said the word carefully, as if it might bite.

Sin.

The category arrived with luggage. Hellfire posters. School corridors. Quiet rooms where people had been told they were stains. Also, unfairly mixed into the same suitcase: cruelty, betrayal, the things that leave other people with less room to breathe. He refused to throw the suitcase out unopened. Sorting was the job.

He rang Joshua while the coffee finished its small miracle. Cars moved in Joshua’s background; life continued to need indicators.

‘If there’s nowhere to banish anyone,’ Michael said, ‘does sin stop existing, or does it just lose its favourite metaphor?’

Joshua did not rush the answer. A kettle somewhere on his end; two households doing the same ordinary sacrament in different rooms of the only room.

‘Sin-as-standing-outside-the-whole can’t complete,’ Joshua said. ‘That’s the paperwork failure. Harm doesn’t need that paperwork to be real. Cruelty is still cruelty when it happens inside the only room there is. Repair is still repair. The fantasy that fails is the clean exile—the idea that you can relocate the unbearable to a metaphysical elsewhere and call the remaining bit pure.’

Michael watched steam. ‘So I’m stuck owning the mess.’

‘You’re stuck belonging to the same field as the mess,’ Joshua said. ‘Including when the mess is you. Including when it’s them. No pure balcony. That’s heavier than a free pass. Anyone who hears “no outside” as “no responsibility” has failed the listening test.’

‘Keep the teeth,’ Joshua said. ‘Lose the landfill. You’re allowed to hate what people do without inventing a second universe to put them in.’

He walked the long way to the shop, not because revelation needed mileage, but because his legs wanted a vote. Along the path a man cursed at a delivery driver for existing in the same lane. The driver’s face did the small collapse of someone absorbing other people’s weather.

Michael felt the old machinery try to fire: *sinner, that one, put him outside the circle of the decent.*

The machinery stalled.

The man was still an idiot. The driver still deserved better. Nothing about unity required Michael to pretend harm was a misunderstanding. What failed was the satisfying fantasy of a fence: pure here, impure there, landfill beyond the parish. The man’s meanness was happening in the same existence as the driver’s flinch and Michael’s judgment and the grey sky that did not take sides with a clipboard.

He bought milk. He held the door for a woman with a pram. He did not award himself a medal. Kindness that needs a medal is just branding.

At home he sat with the notebook open under last night’s three lines and added a fourth set, hand slower than his thoughts:

**Sin-as-elsewhere: misfiled.**  
**Harm-as-real: not optional.**  
**Repair-without-exile: the actual work.**

Cameron arrived mid-afternoon with two coffees and the face of a man who had read a long message and come to check the patient in person.

‘I was about to write a very sharp text,’ Cameron said, handing over a cup, ‘about people who discover monism and immediately become arseholes. I laminated it. I’m keeping it in draft forever.’

‘Please do,’ Michael said. ‘I need the threat more than the send.’

Cameron sat on the edge of the table, practical as a spirit level. ‘So: no exit hatch for the wicked. Still wicked, though. Still repair. Still don’t be the arsehole.’

‘That’s the translation,’ Michael said. ‘Less theatre. More inside. I keep wanting to put people outside and finding there’s only more inside.’

‘That’s the category cracking,’ Cameron said. ‘Don’t replace it with mush. Keep the teeth. Lose the landfill. Joshua’s line, I assume. I’m only the courier of sanity.’

Michael thought of private lists—apologies owed, sharpness he had called honesty, the small abandonments that do not make sermons and still thin a life. Those were not problems for a separate universe. They were work for this one.

He did not feel holy. He felt adult in the unglamorous sense: the sense that cancels the holiday of pure elsewhere and keeps the calendar of repair. Adult, here, did not mean joyless. It meant the jokes had to carry weight, and the weight had to allow jokes.

Evening, he washed the plate again. Same plate. Same hands. Same only room. Cameron had gone; Joshua had texted once—*How’s the air?*—and Michael had answered truthfully: still no hatch, less theatre.

The category called sin, stripped of its exit fantasy, looked less like a cosmic stain chart and more like a set of verbs that still needed doing: stop, mend, refuse cruelty, tell the truth without enjoying the wound.

No outside left.

Therefore no sin-as-banishment.

Therefore—only therefore—more honesty about what still hurts people here.

He turned off the light. The dark did not exile the day. It continued it.

Tomorrow could argue about planks and eyes. Tonight the argument was simpler, and harder:

There was nowhere left to throw the parts of the story he could not stand.

Including himself.
