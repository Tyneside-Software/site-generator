# Chapter 34
## Recruiter At The Fork

The email arrived with the cheerful professionalism of a man who got paid to open doors other people would call destinies.

Subject line polite. Body clean. Six-figure CTO. PE-backed. Near enough to home that the geography would not even punish him. AI, SaaS, automation, executive team, direct reports, growth phase—the whole modern hymn sheet. A recruiter named Tom, who had done his homework well enough to make flattery feel like research.

Michael read it once at the kitchen table with coffee.

He read it again standing up, as if posture might change the moral physics.

Outside, a bin lorry performed its sacramental racket. Inside, the early-stage thing he was building—local, awkward, still smelling of paint and promise—sat in his mind like a plant that had only just put down roots and would not survive being forgotten for a season of someone else’s exit timeline.

Easy path. Hard path.

He hated how cartoon the fork looked when he named it. Life was supposed to be more sophisticated than a morality play. And yet here was the play, emailed at 09:12, with a calendar link.

Joshua came round later with pastries that were an apology for being late and also just pastries. Sugar on the paper bag. The smell of butter that had survived a short walk.

“Read this,” Michael said.

Joshua read. Whistled once, soft. “That’s a real door.”

“It’s a broad path with excellent lighting,” Michael said. “I could walk through it and call it provision.”

“Could,” Joshua agreed. “Would it still be your life?”

Michael sat. The chair creaked in the vernacular of cheap furniture everywhere. “If I take the easy route I won’t half-take it. I’ll go all in. Comfort. Status. Enough money to forget what I was trying to build. That’s the trap of me—all or nothing.”

Joshua tore a pastry and did not offer empty comfort. Flakes fell onto the table like small irrelevant snow. “All-or-nothing is an engine. It’s also a cliff with nice branding.”

“I’m not looking for prep,” Michael said. “I’m looking at the fork. Live for the work that costs me, or live for the life that pays me to stop meaning it.”

His phone lit. Cameron, who had been looped in because friends are sometimes the quality-assurance department of the soul:

Cameron: *Confidential chat with a recruiter is information, not marriage.*

Michael: *I know. Still a fork.*

Cameron: *Forks can be walked past without declaring one road dead forever. You’re dramatic when hungry.*

Michael almost typed something sharp, then ate a pastry instead. Cameron was annoying in the specific way that keeps people alive.

Joshua said, “The narrow path only stays narrow if you keep choosing it on purpose. Not if you fuse your entire self to unbroken forward motion so hard that any pause feels like death.”

“If I stop I don’t know who I am,” Michael said, and heard how true and how dangerous the sentence was.

“You’re the one who chooses,” Joshua said. “The values were in you before this vehicle had a name. They’ll be in you if the shape changes. Don’t make annihilation the price of rest, and don’t make the PE job the only alternative to holiness. Those are not the only two towns on the map.”

Michael looked at the email signature again. Tom looked like a nice enough bloke. The job looked like a life many good people would take without shame. That was what made it hard. Evil would have been easier to refuse.

He thought of cleaners paid properly, of new parents, of ward-by-ward stubbornness. He thought of the robot life of earning comfort while the inner vocation starved—and how that robot life felt, to him, worse than difficulty.

“I’m staying on the hard path,” he said at last. Not as theatre. As a decision that still had to be remade tomorrow.

Joshua nodded. “Then answer Tom like a free man, not a martyr. Polite. Clear. No drama. Keep your eyes open about runway and sleep. Martyrs who don’t sleep become problems for the people they meant to serve.”

Michael drafted the reply at the table. The first version had too much soul. The second had too little gratitude. The third left the thanks, closed the door without slamming it, and did not use the word *destiny* once.

Send.

The fork did not vanish. Forks rarely do. They just stop pretending to be the whole landscape.

He washed the coffee mugs. Joshua stole the last pastry with fraternal immunity and looked entirely without remorse.

Outside, the bin lorry moved on to the next street, efficient as empire, ordinary as Tuesday. A neighbour waved. Michael waved back with wet hands.

He checked the sent folder once—not for drama, for the adult pleasure of a decision that had used language carefully. No martyr essay. No contempt for Tom. No secret clause that the hard path meant self-erasure. Just: thank you, not now, building something local.

Joshua stood to go, brushing sugar from his sleeve. “You all right?”

“Lighter,” Michael said. “Which is suspicious.”

“Suspicious and welcome,” Joshua said. “That’s usually the right pair.”

At the door Joshua paused. “And if Tom writes again with something even prettier?”

“Same fork,” Michael said. “Same feet. Same choice.”

Joshua grinned. “Good. Keep choosing on purpose. Accidental holiness is just inertia with a halo.”

When the door closed, Michael stood in the kitchen alone with the crumbs and the quiet. The plant in his mind—awkward, local, still smelling of paint—had not been uprooted for someone else’s exit timeline.

He laughed once, short, at himself and at Tom and at the cartoon fork that had turned out to be real after all.

Then he opened the laptop and went back to the hard path, which looked, from this angle, a lot like work.
