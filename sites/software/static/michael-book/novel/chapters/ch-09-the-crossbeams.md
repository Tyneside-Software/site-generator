# Chapter 9
## The Crossbeams

Wednesday evening Joshua came over with a bag of shopping and a length of string.

‘Don’t look at me like that,’ he said, dumping apples on the counter. ‘The string is a teaching aid. The apples are dinner-adjacent. You look like a man who has been bonding with delimiters.’

‘Load-bearing punctuation,’ Michael said.

‘Of course.’

They ate late—eggs, toast, apples cut with the seriousness of surgeons. Conversation stayed light on purpose: a neighbour’s new dog, a client who wrote emails like ransom notes, the eternal mystery of why bread always lands butter-side down in theory and usually not in practice. Joshua waited until the plates were empty before he reached for the string. Timing, Michael was learning, was half of teaching.

Joshua cleared a space on the table and laid the string down in a straight line.

‘Vertical,’ he said. ‘Up-down. Heaven and earth language. Hierarchy. The bit of us that wants a skyhook and a ground.’

He laid a second piece of string across it.

‘Horizontal. Side-to-side. Neighbour. Chronos. The washing-up. The person next to you on the bus who exists whether you have a theory about them or not.’

The cross sat on the wood, humble as a plus sign that had been to church. The overhead bulb made a small shadow of it, a softer copy of the same claim.

Michael felt something in his chest lean forward. ‘You’re going to talk about Rome.’

‘I’m going to talk about Rome. Because if we only do clever geometry we get a logo. Rome did not invent the cross for logos. They used it for death with paperwork. Public. Slow. Designed to attach shame to a body so thoroughly that the body became a warning sign for other bodies.’

The fridge hummed. Outside, someone dragged a bin to the kerb with a sound like a small reluctant animal.

‘So when people wear it now,’ Michael said carefully, ‘they’re wearing an execution method that got… what? Rebranded?’

‘Reinvented. That’s the wild claim at the centre. Not that wood stopped being wood. That a tool of humiliation got walked through a story so strange it came out the other side meaning *this is how love looks when it refuses to leave the room*. You don’t have to buy the whole theology tonight. You only have to notice the shape: vertical claim about God-and-world, horizontal claim about us-and-us, and the join where they hurt.’

Michael touched the intersection with one finger. The string shifted a millimetre and then held.

‘I’ve been collecting bricks. Experience that won’t unplug. No pure nothing. One room. No edge. Everything fits without being blessed. Both/and instead of street-forks. Maths-words-things in one file. And now you’re saying the next brick is a Roman punishment that became a symbol of—what—presence?’

‘Of not escaping. Of the sacred not getting a private jet out of the ordinary. Of the vertical not abandoning the horizontal when the horizontal gets bloody. People turn it into jewellery and tribal paint and I understand why that makes you itch. Under the itch, though, there’s a structure your whole week has been leaning toward: up-down and side-to-side fastened at a cost.’

Michael sat back. He thought of the Chair of Non-Being. Of doors into more here. Of the preacher and the sceptic, each gripping one beam and calling it the whole cross. Of Joshua saying *horizontal beam* about dishes and meaning more than dishes.

‘I can take the structure. The geometry is clean. The history is ugly in a way that feels honest. What I can’t do is make it merely clever. If I do, I’m just another man with a diagram and a dry heart.’

Joshua’s face did something soft and fierce at once. ‘Good. That’s the door I wanted you to find yourself. Clever is a beam. Love is what the beams are for. Without that, you’ve built a plus sign and called it a cathedral.’

They sat with the string cross between them like a third guest who did not need a drink. Michael made tea. The kettle’s click was domestic and absolute. Joshua washed the plates without being asked, horizontal work in a kitchen that still smelled faintly of eggs. The ordinary continued its policy of being the only stage available.

‘Rome was efficient,’ Michael said eventually. ‘Ashamed, too, in the way empires are ashamed under the armour—ashamed enough to need spectacles of control. And then the story says the spectacle got stolen from them. Not by an army. By a meaning they couldn’t execute hard enough to kill.’

‘That’s the claim. Reinvention so thorough it offends people who liked their symbols either purely pious or purely cynical. Both/And shop again. Death *and* life. Shame *and* honour flipped. Wood *and* world.’

Michael photographed the string on the table before they tidied it away—not for social media, for the museum of the week. In the notebook he left one line only, spoken first so the ink would not get ideas: *Do not let this stay merely clever.*

Joshua heard it. ‘Frame that one.’

‘I’m putting it next to the empty data field. Reserved seat. Something belongs there that isn’t a slogan yet.’

When Joshua left, the table was just a table again. Michael stood in the kitchen and held his hands out as if measuring: up, across, join.

The flat did not become a church.

It did become, for a moment, a workshop in which a man was allowed to handle dangerous shapes carefully, without either sneering or kneeling on command—

—and that, he thought, was a kind of honesty the week could use.
