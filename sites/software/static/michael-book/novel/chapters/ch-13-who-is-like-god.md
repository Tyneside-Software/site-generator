# Chapter 13
## Who Is Like God?

Friday evening the flat was full of the smell of toast and the particular silence that follows a week of thinking too loudly.

Michael had the slender book open on the table and was not reading it. He was stuck on his own name, which is a strange place to be stuck, like tripping over the doormat of yourself.

Joshua arrived with a bottle of something soft and fizzy because the week did not need wine’s opinions. He took one look at Michael’s face and said, ‘You’ve found etymology. I know that face. It’s the face of a man who has googled himself in Hebrew and is now having feelings.’

‘Michael,’ Michael said. ‘Who is like God? That’s the question form. A name that is not an answer. A name that is a shove away from the throne.’

Joshua sat. ‘And?’

‘And parents hand it out like a jumper. Happy Christmas, son: rhetorical monotheism. No pressure.’

Joshua grinned. ‘Better than being named after a car.’

‘I’m serious. All week I’ve been building—careful bricks, vows about cleverness, empty fields for other people’s symbols—and then I remember my own label is a warning label. *Who is like God?* Implied answer: not you, mate. Not the archangel either, not really—you’re named *after* the question the archangel’s name *is*. You’re a souvenir mug from a cosmic argument.’

Joshua poured the fizz. Bubbles rose with industrious joy. ‘You’re not the archangel. Good. Archangels have terrible hours and very little downtime. You’re a man who washes up and laughs on buses. The name isn’t a job offer. It’s a posture.’

‘Posture,’ Michael repeated.

‘Chin not quite so high. Knees able to bend. The question keeps a gap open where idolatry wants to weld you shut. *Who is like God?* If you answer *me* with a straight face, you’ve failed the exam. If you answer *no one* and then go about your business with a bit of awe left in the tank, you’ve understood the nickname.’

Michael rubbed his face. ‘It sits oddly next to the week. Experience that won’t unplug—almost feels like a throne room. One room, no outside—claustrophobic grandeur. Everything fits—totalising. Then the crossbeams—easy to cast yourself as the clever joiner of heaven and earth. The name is a bucket of water.’

‘Good. You needed a bucket. I was going to throw one eventually.’

They drank. The toast cooled into the edible form of regret; Michael made more. Ordinary salvage.

‘When we were kids,’ Joshua said, ‘you used to introduce yourself like the name was a dare. *Michael.* Full. As if daring the room to see an angel and finding a boy with scraped knees. I always liked that. You weren’t pretending to be celestial. You were stubborn about being present.’

‘Present is the axiom,’ Michael said softly. ‘Start here. Not start on a cloud.’

‘Exactly. Who-is-like-God is not *you-are-nothing*. It’s *you-are-not-the-whole*, which is what makes room for love. If you were the whole, there’d be no horizontal beam—no neighbour, no brother, no stranger with a crescent on a book spine you refuse to steal. The question protects relation.’

Michael set the notebook aside without filling a page. The vow was easier spoken than framed: not a promotion, a permanent open question; not the archangel, not the whole; the gap where other people fit, and where worship does not collapse into mirror-work.

Joshua watched him not write. ‘That’s better than a wall of rules. Walls are for hanging tools. The work is elsewhere.’

‘I’m going to need a life,’ Michael said.

‘You’re going to need a life,’ Joshua agreed. ‘And toast that isn’t burned. Baby steps.’

They moved to easier talk for a while: a funny email from a client, a film Joshua half-recommended and then immediately half-unrecommended, the neighbour’s door that always slammed as if making a point about ontology. Second toast arrived. Butter did its quiet work. The evening softened the way evenings do when nobody is trying to win them. Michael felt the name settle from alarm back into clothing—something he wore, not something that wore him.

At the door, as Joshua left, Michael said, ‘If I start acting like the answer to my own name, you’ll tell me.’

‘I’ll do worse. I’ll make you dry every plate in the house while I recite the question in different accents.’

‘That’s abuse.’

‘That’s love with props.’

After the door closed, Michael stood in the hall—the same hall that had refused to lead outside of here—and said his name once, quietly, like a man checking a tool.

‘Michael.’

Not a throne. Not a joke. A question that kept the ceiling from coming down and crushing the room where brothers drank fizz and ate second toast.

He slept without diagrams.

In the morning there would be a text, and a footnote about rakes, and the end of something that was only the first act—

—but Friday night belonged to the gap in the name, and the mercy of not being God, and the strange relief of answering the ancient question with a life instead of a speech.
