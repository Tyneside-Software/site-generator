# Chapter 17
## Prediction Errors For Breakfast

Tuesday morning Michael expected jam.

He had set the jar out the night before with the confidence of a man who believed the future was a continuation of the past. Toast rose. Butter softened. He opened the jar, spread what should have been strawberry, and tasted orange.

Marmalade.

The shock was small, domestic, and total. For half a second the kitchen reorganised itself around a wrongness no larger than a teaspoon. Then the taste became itself, and the wrongness became information.

His phone, still open from last night's half-read article, helpfully reminded him that brains ran on prediction. You lived inside a model. The world was allowed to disagree. Surprise was the bill for being wrong in public.

Michael stood with the toast halfway to his mouth and felt the contact land.

Not metaphor. Contact.

The model had said jam. Reality had said citrus peel and sugar. The gap between them was not a bug in the universe; it was the universe tapping him on the shoulder.

Joshua rang as Michael was still prosecuting the toast for identity fraud.

'You sound cheerful,' Joshua said.

'I was wrong about jam. It improved me.'

'That's the whole game. Wrongness with flavour. People try to live without prediction errors and call it peace. Mostly it's just a closed tab.'

'And the open tab?'

'Is breakfast. And brothers. And whatever the afternoon invents that you didn't put in the calendar.'

Michael hung up and ate. The toast was good once he stopped cross-examining it. The second bite carried less drama. The third was almost ordinary. That, too, was the system working: surprise, revise, settle, until the next betrayal of expectation.

He thought of the podcast brain from yesterday—the late dessert, the furniture argument. Prediction-error talk sat in the same house. You could describe the nervous system as a forecasting office forever sending memos to itself. Fine. Useful. Bill-paying science. But the memo still arrived *as* something it was like to be corrected. The error was not a number in a vault. It was orange where red should have been.

At the café near work the barista called a name that was not his. Three people looked up. Two sat down again, disappointed in a way that cost nothing. One collected the wrong cup, realised, laughed, swapped. Tiny prediction errors, cascading through a room full of models with milk preferences.

Michael ordered tea because he predicted tea would help, and it did, which was either accurate forecasting or a self-fulfilling prophecy wearing a teabag.

A woman at the next table dropped a spoon. The clink rewrote the soundscape for everyone within earshot. No one called it philosophy. Everyone updated.

He remembered arguing, years ago, that he hated surprises—parties, sudden news, plot twists in films he had paid to control. Looking back, he had not hated surprise. He had hated *unmanaged* surprise—the kind that arrived without a handle. Marmalade was a handle. It was the world being precise.

Inside the office a meeting ran long. Someone used a slide deck that predicted agreement and received silence. The silence was a prediction error with PowerPoint. Michael felt a flicker of kindness for the presenter. Being wrong in front of people was just marmalade with higher stakes.

By lunch he had nearly sent a sharp email, predicted the satisfaction of being right, and then imagined the other person's face receiving it. He rewrote the email three times until the surprise he was planning for them was usefulness instead of heat. That, too, was a model update: *I am the sort of man who can choose which error to introduce into someone else's morning.*

He thought of friendships that had surprised him—people kinder than the forecast, people colder, people who arrived with marmalade when he had stocked his heart with jam. Prediction error as intimacy. Not always pleasant. Always information.

In the afternoon he bought an actual jam doughnut, out of loyalty to the old forecast, and found it exactly as advertised. The universe, occasionally, confirmed you. That was contact too—quieter, easier to miss, still real. He ate it on a low wall outside the office, sugar on his thumb, traffic performing its usual chaos with the confidence of a system that never read the article.

A pigeon landed, predicted crumbs, received none, left without filing a complaint. Even pigeons ran forecasting offices. Even pigeons updated.

Joshua appeared at the end of the day as if summoned by citrus, two coffees in hand, and sat on the wall beside him.

'Still tasting orange?' Joshua asked.

'Only when I check. The model has filed an amendment.'

'Good. Don't worship the amendment either. Models that never get surprised are just statues with opinions.'

They watched a van reverse into a space it would not fit, revise, reverse again, succeed by two centimetres of pure humility. Michael raised his cup in silent salute.

Week two was teaching him systems without letting systems eat the meal.

The meal was still happening.

It just sometimes tasted of orange.
