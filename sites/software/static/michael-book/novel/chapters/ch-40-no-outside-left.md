# Chapter 40
## No Outside Left

The crisis did not arrive with thunder.

It arrived on a grey midweek afternoon while Michael was washing a plate, which is how the serious ones prefer to travel—under the cover of chores.

Soap, water, the small circular work of a sponge. Outside, the sky could not decide between rain and boredom. Inside, the week’s noticings stacked without his permission into something that felt less like a list and more like a wall.

Experience that would not unplug. Nothing that failed as an alternative. Rooms whose doors opened into more of the same here. Maps without a true edge. Systems that kept joining the museum of everything. Both/and as shop sign. Sandpaper friends. Optional logic doors. Masks. Forks. Wolves that were families. Letters that were and were not. Fonts with unfinished mischief. Wood that killed and shamed and then got stolen by love.

Individually they were thoughts.

Together they did something rude to the idea of elsewhere.

If there is no true outside—if every exit is still inside the only field—then where do you put the bin bag of the cosmos? Where do you throw the part you cannot stand? Where does hell live, if hell is eternal separation and separation cannot finish its paperwork?

Michael’s hands went still in the soapy water. A bubble on his knuckle held a tiny warped kitchen, then burst.

The feeling was not enlightenment. Enlightenment, in the brochures, comes with soft lighting. This felt like claustrophobia with a philosophy degree. Also—traitorously—like relief.

No outside left.

He dried his hands without drying them properly and walked to the back door as if fresh air could reinstall a beyond. The garden was small. The sky was large. Neither was elsewhere in the sense he meant. Sky was still experience. Garden was still here. Even the fantasy of escape was happening *as* a thought in a skull that had never found the hatch behind consciousness.

He texted Joshua a single line:

Michael: *No outside left. Panicking politely.*

Joshua rang within a minute. “Talk.”

“If there’s no true cut,” Michael said, pacing the kitchen tiles, “then there’s nowhere to put the rejected bits. No final exile. No clean hell as a separate estate. I’m stuck with the whole. We’re stuck with the whole. It’s—God, Josh, it’s airless and it’s the first breath at the same time.”

Joshua was quiet long enough to be useful. “Yes.”

“That’s not a comfort.”

“It’s not a slogan either,” Joshua said. “It’s a crisis because your old escape hatches were load-bearing. Sin-as-somewhere-else. Failure-as-outside-the-story. The fantasy that the unbearable can be relocated to a metaphysical landfill. If the landfill is still on the map of being, the rubbish doesn’t leave. It transforms the problem.”

Michael sat on the floor with his back against the cupboards, adult long legs folded like a bad camping chair. The fridge hummed. Somewhere upstairs a pipe ticked. The ordinary world continued its policy of not waiting for metaphysical clearance.

“I feel trapped.”

“You feel undivided,” Joshua said. “Trapped is what undivided feels like when you still wanted a trapdoor.”

Cameron messaged into the group, sensing weather:

Cameron: *You two sound like a cult again. Translate.*

Michael: *If existence has no true outside, eternal separation is incoherent. Hell-as-exit fails. Also I can’t leave my own life by philosophy. Having a moment.*

Cameron: *Okay. That’s actually heavy. Still brush your teeth. Ontology doesn’t cancel dinner.*

Michael laughed, helpless. The laugh cracked the airlessness just enough. Soap-slick hands left a print on his jeans; he did not care.

Joshua heard it. “There. Ordinary returns. Keep the crisis; lose the melodrama. No outside left means you don’t get to outsource repair. It also means nothing is finally thrown away from the field—including you when you fail, including them when they sleep, including the parts of the story that disgust you. The work gets more intimate. Not smaller. Closer.”

Michael looked at the bin in the corner—actual landfill’s little household apostle—and felt the metaphor try to get clever. He refused it. The point was simpler.

There was no balcony outside existence from which to judge existence.

There was only more here.

He said, “I’m scared of what that does to guilt. To blame. To the category people call sin.”

“Good,” Joshua said. “Scared means you’re not using it as a free pass yet. Next chapters can argue. Tonight you just survive the first sight of the closed map.”

After the call Michael finished the washing-up like a monk with a sink. Plate, mug, the knife that always hid under the foam. He ate toast that was slightly burnt and called it dinner. He stood in the doorway of the bedroom and watched ordinary sleep happen in the next room without demanding it become a symbol—breath, a shifted shoulder, the small mercy of someone else being safely elsewhere-in-the-house while still entirely here.

No outside left.

Claustrophobic.

Free.

Both—because of course both—the shop never closed, even in a crisis.

He turned off the kitchen light. The dark was not outside the day. It was the day continuing by other means.

Michael went to bed inside the only room there is, and for the first time the phrase *the only room* felt less like a slogan and more like a sentence with consequences—consequences that would start asking what sin even means when there is nowhere left to banish it to.

That question could wait until morning. Morning would still be here.

He lay still and let the airlessness and the first breath share the same lungs until sleep took both of them without asking which was which.
