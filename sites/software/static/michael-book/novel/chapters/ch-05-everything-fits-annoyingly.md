# Chapter 5
## Everything Fits, Annoyingly

Tuesday began with a glove.

Not a vision. Not a metaphor arranged by angels with a budget. A glove—black, slightly damp, abandoned on the low wall by the bus stop as if its owner had been mid-thought and simply evaporated into a better appointment.

Michael stopped. Rain had left a bead of water on the knuckle. The thumb was folded under. Useful things, mislaid, still doing the shape of usefulness with nobody inside.

He took a photograph. He was not sure why. Perhaps because the morning after a night of no-edge philosophy leaves a man hungry for small, finite objects with obvious jobs.

On the bus he sat opposite a teenager with headphones large enough to qualify as architecture. An advert for cheap broadband promised a faster life. Someone’s shopping bag leaked the smell of bananas. All of it filed itself without asking permission: sound, colour, irritation, the faint pride of having a theory before nine a.m.

At his desk—laptop open, coffee cooling, the small republic of tabs that constitutes modern work—he tried to find something that would not fit.

This is a mug’s game, and he knew it, but mugs sometimes find things.

Nightmares fit: they are experiences with bad reviews.  
Boredom fits: it has a texture, a drag, a clock.  
Other people’s joy, which he could not feel from the inside, fit as *not-feeling*—as rumour, as a story about a room he was not currently standing in.  
Maths fit as thought, as marks on screens, as the peculiar pleasure of a proof clicking shut.  
Evil he approached carefully, the way you pick up a pan you suspect is hot. Evil fit as harm, as choice, as the sick weight in the stomach when you remember what humans do to each other. Fitting did not mean *excused*. Fitting meant *not outside the field*. You do not exile cruelty into a second universe to keep your metaphysics tidy; you keep it in this one where responsibility still has an address.

Joshua rang at lunch. Traffic murmured behind him; he was walking somewhere with purpose.

‘You’re doing the everything-fits face,’ Joshua said. ‘I can hear it. It’s the face of a man who has realised the universe doesn’t have a lost property office for inconvenient facts.’

‘There’s a glove on a wall,’ Michael said. ‘It belongs. The photo belongs. My annoyance that I photographed a glove belongs. Even the bit of me that wants a special exception for something—anything—belongs.’

‘Annoying, isn’t it?’

‘Deeply. I wanted a trapdoor. A category labelled *does not apply*. Instead I get a museum that accepts donations twenty-four hours a day and never issues receipts.’

Joshua was quiet for a moment. A car horn somewhere; life continuing its policy of not waiting for theories to finish. ‘All-encompassing doesn’t mean all-approved,’ he said. ‘That’s the mistake people make when they hear “everything is in.” They think you’re blessing the contents. You’re not. You’re describing the building. The building can contain a fire. That doesn’t make fire a houseplant.’

Michael looked at the glove photo again. Ordinary. Complete. Indifferent to his theories and yet perfectly willing to sit inside them.

‘So the whole is not missing a piece called nothing,’ he said slowly, ‘and not missing a porch, and not missing a rim—and also not missing a bin for the bits we don’t like. It keeps them. That’s the annoying part. The honesty of it.’

‘Write it down before you get noble about it,’ Joshua said. ‘Noble is where good ideas go to wear uncomfortable shoes.’

Michael wrote in the notebook:

**Everything that shows up, shows up here.**  
**Fitting is not blessing.**  
**The museum is not a church. (Or: if it is, the sermons are optional and the exhibits are not.)**

He worked through the afternoon with the peculiar lightness of a man who has stopped looking for the emergency exit. Emails were emails. A spreadsheet behaved like a spreadsheet, which is to say it pretended to be neutral while quietly judging his formulae. At three he made more coffee. At four he remembered the sandwich Joshua had bullied him into packing and ate it over the keyboard like a civilised animal.

Near five, a colleague—not Cameron, not yet a full character in this story, only a voice from a doorway—asked if he was all right, because he had been smiling at nothing.

‘Not nothing,’ Michael said, and then, because the truth was too large for a corridor, ‘Just a glove.’

The colleague nodded the nod of people who have decided not to enquire further, which is one of the quiet kindnesses of workplaces.

On the bus home the wall was empty. The glove had gone—claimed, binned, or promoted to a better narrative. Michael felt a brief, absurd grief, then laughed under his breath.

Of course it was gone. Going was also a kind of fitting. Absence-of-this-glove was not pure nothing; it was a new arrangement of the same room, with a small cold patch on a wall where wool had been.

That evening Joshua turned up at the flat with a bag of chips and the air of a man who had decided philosophy needed salt.

‘Pub tomorrow,’ Joshua said, dumping vinegar like a sacrament. ‘I have beer mats and a bad idea.’

Michael took a chip. Hot. Real. Fitting, annoyingly, like everything else.

‘Bring the bad idea,’ he said. ‘I’ll bring the annoyance.’

Outside, the streetlights came on one by one, filing themselves into the museum without ceremony. Michael did not bless them. He did not need to. They were already in.
