# Chapter 97
## The Capstone Problem

The floating tip returned in a dream: Michael placing a little pyramid on a big one while friends’ faces flattened into bricks under his hands, smiling as if being load-bearing were a compliment. He woke sour, mouth dry, heart still climbing a structure that did not exist. He went for a run that punished the image into sweat and pavement. A fox watched him from a garden like a critic who had not been paid. He did not ask the fox for a blurb.

At breakfast he told Harriet. She buttered toast with judicial calm, the knife doing more honest work than most sermons.

‘Capstone problem,’ she said. ‘Who finishes the shape, who claims the view, who gets buried in the base. You’ve been demoting yourself from peak for weeks. Good. Don’t sneak back up via “I’m the man who refuses peaks.” That’s a peak with better PR. Anti-peak is still a brand if you sell it. Brands need customers. Don’t make your friends into customers for your humility.’

He winced into coffee. ‘Caught. The dream was a performance review with worse lighting.’

‘Dreams are free consultants,’ Harriet said. ‘Expensive if you ignore them. Eat your toast.’

Joshua met him at the canal with two coffees and no children’s Egypt book this time—advanced level, adult mud, swans conducting foreign policy near the bank with the confidence of diplomats who can bite.

‘Capstone problem is structural,’ Joshua said. ‘Movements want finishers. Brands want logos on the tip. You want your recognition to matter without becoming the tip of other people’s lives. So: refuse the job whenever it’s offered—including by your own mirror. Especially by your own mirror. Mirrors hire badly. Mirrors promote on charisma.’

Michael watched a swan hiss at a tourist who had believed in bread against all signage. The tourist retreated, re-educated. ‘R.’s seal looks like a capstone to my old eyes. I’ve learned it’s community protection, different object. My monogram looks like a capstone to the dinner man who wanted a fight. To me it’s a joke in a wallet. Perspective is a hall of tips. Everyone thinks someone else’s completion is arrogance, and their own is weather.’

‘Then don’t trust your felt sense of “I’m only being humble,”’ Joshua said. ‘Trust behaviour. Do you interrupt more or less? Do you need credit? Do you vanish when you’re not the interesting one? Do you let Cameron have the last word without a both/and encore that re-centres you as the reasonable adult?’

Michael ran an honest audit while coffee cooled and disliked some line items. After they parted he sent two short messages for two small throne-grabs in group chats—apologies without theatre. Cost: mild embarrassment. Benefit: spine. One person replied *no worries*; the other did not reply, which was also data, and data that did not require a monologue about how hard it is to be good.

R. joined them later on a bench when her calendar spat out twenty free minutes, scarf against wind, eyes clear. ‘Capstone problem in interfaith: everyone wants to be the final interpreter of everyone else. Solution: don’t. Be a brick. Bricks are allowed to be beautiful. They’re not allowed to pretend they’re the sky. Sky doesn’t need your CV. Sky was doing sky before your monogram.’

Cameron, remote as weather, texted into the group: *Also: unfinished pyramids are honest. The gap you drew is a feature. Live in the draft. Drafts don’t get statues. Good. Statues get pigeon opinions.*

Michael laughed, surprising himself, the sound scattering a couple of sparrows. ‘Draft theology. I can live there. Statues collect reviews from birds who don’t care about your system.’

‘All theology is draft if you’re not dead,’ R. said. ‘And even then, people edit you. Try not to make their job easy by being a tyrant in life. Tyrants are simple to summarise. Living people are harder, which is a kindness to biographers and a duty to friends.’

Her timer buzzed—modern calligraphy for *enough*. Before she left she poked Michael’s shoulder. ‘If you ever publish, no cover with you on a mountain. Cover with a table. Or jam. Or a keyboard that stays a keyboard. Or potatoes from the dinner where you didn’t win. Winning is a terrible aesthetic.’

‘Jam,’ Michael said solemnly. ‘Denise would approve. Harriet would demand royalties for the toast and a percentage of any metaphor.’

Alone later, he helped Harriet with a flat-pack shelf—the most anti-peak activity available this side of laundry. Instructions in five languages, each slightly lying. One missing screw, which is how the universe keeps philosophers employed. Laughter that bent them double when a panel went on backwards and looked briefly like modern art. A shelf that held books without becoming a metaphor until Michael almost made it one and Harriet said, ‘Don’t. Let it be a shelf. The books can be pretentious without the furniture joining in. We need somewhere for the novels and the cookbook. That’s the whole of the law for this evening.’

He let it be a shelf. He put novels on it, and a cookbook with jam-stained corners, and nothing that claimed to finish God. Harriet hung a picture slightly crooked on purpose, then straightened it, human, ordinary, the opposite of a floating tip.

The capstone problem did not vanish. Problems that live in prestige never vanish; they apply for promotions under new names—anti-peak, final interpreter, the man who refuses thrones so loudly that the refusal becomes a throne.

Evening closed. Michael stood in the doorway of the room with the new shelf and felt no urge to stand on it. Outside, the fox may or may not have been judging him; foxes have their own capstones, mostly involving bins.

He washed two mugs. He texted R. a photograph of the shelf, captioned only: *Not a mountain.*

She replied: *Acceptable. Now live underneath it without climbing.*

He would try. The funny old life, unfinished on purpose, left the tip off so the air could still move—and so friends’ faces could stay faces, not bricks, not base, not the price of someone else’s shape.
