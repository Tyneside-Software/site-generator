# Chapter 86
## Cyclical Realities

The washing machine entered spin and Michael’s thoughts, which had been pretending to be a straight corridor with a trophy at the end, went round with it.

Glass, water, shirts. A sock flashed past like a minor prophet with no congregation and too much velocity. Final chapters, bigger books, seals, open pages—all of it had been living in his head as a hallway you walked once and then graduated from. The drum suggested otherwise: same cotton, new dirt, clean again, dirt again. No certificate. Only soap and gravity and the ugly honesty of a programme called *cotton 40*.

Joshua arrived with croissants and found him standing there with tea gone cold, face lit by the little window of the machine.

‘You’ve been hypnotised by laundry,’ Joshua said. ‘I respect that. Laundry never lies about how long it will take.’

‘Cycles,’ Michael said, still watching the sock. ‘I keep treating recognition like a once-for-all graduation. Then Tuesday arrives and I’m jealous of a stranger’s parking space, or petty about the dishes, or tired in a way that makes me cruel, and I think I’ve fallen out of the truth. What if the truth includes the spin cycle?’

Joshua put pastry on a plate, brushed flakes onto the counter, and refused to apologise to the counter. Crumbs were evidence of eating, not failure of hospitality.

‘Linear pride hates cycles,’ he said. ‘It wants a conversion date and a flat graph of improvement that only goes up and to the right, like a share price designed by an optimist. People are seasons. Faith is seasons. Even funny old life has weather, not a permanent climate-controlled revelation with complimentary robes.’

They ate standing up. Outside, rain started, stopped, started again—the sky doing laundry without shame. Something in Michael’s shoulders loosened: permission to relearn kindness without being expelled from his own life.

Later they walked to the park because R. had ten minutes between errands, which is how real friendship often schedules metaphysics—a bench, a bus timetable, no retreat centre. A dog chased the same stick into the same shallow river with undiminished joy, shook itself, and waited for the throw again as if novelty were a rumour invented by people with money.

Michael pointed. ‘That dog is my spiritual director today.’

R. smiled, scarf tucked, bag at her feet. ‘Islam has a calendar that returns,’ she said when he floated the spin cycle properly, not as poetry but as a problem he had been having with himself. ‘Fasts return. Feasts return. You don’t graduate from needing the year. Christians have liturgical rounds too, when they remember. The danger is thinking *cycle* means nothing matters—hamster wheel, shrug forever. Cycle can mean keep practising because you are a creature. Creatures get dirty. Creatures wash. Creatures do not get a permanent diploma that exempts them from Tuesday.’

She checked the time—not as insult, as creaturehood. ‘I have to go. Same bench next week if the weather allows. That’s a cycle too. Don’t make it a shrine. Make it a habit that can survive rain.’

When she left, they watched the dog complete another lap of pure, unembarrassed repetition, then took the long way home along the canal. The water was never the same water. The path was the same path. A runner lapped them, breathing like a metronome that had found religion. Both true: change and return.

At his desk he wrote nothing grand. He paid a bill he paid every month. He stretched the hip that complained when he pretended he was twenty-five. He watered the plant he usually forgot until it staged a crisis. He did not ask the monogram in his wallet to be new. He asked it to be *again*—usable on a dull afternoon when no song was playing and no one was watching.

Evening, Joshua phoned while Michael folded shirts still warm from the machine.

‘How’s the cycle?’

‘Less shame when I relearn the same kindness,’ Michael said. He shook a sleeve by the cuff, ordinary ritual. ‘I used to think repetition meant I failed the final exam. Now it might mean I’m alive. Also I still snap at bad drivers. Recognition didn’t delete me.’

‘Good,’ Joshua said. ‘Deleted people can’t love. Slightly damp people can. Amen to damp.’

Before bed Michael put another load on—towels this time, the humble cloud. The machine sang its ugly song. He stood with one hand on the plastic lid and thought of α==ω not as a finish line but as a shape you could walk around year after year, seeing both readings depending on the light, forgetting, remembering, washing the same mug.

He did not take notes. He turned the dial and pressed start, which is a kind of amen if you do it without an audience.

The towels tumbled. Outside, a fox crossed the road with freelance confidence and vanished into a hedge that had seen every season and would see them again. Michael switched off the kitchen light and left the machine to its work—round and round, clean enough for another day of ordinary dirt—

—and for the first time in weeks, the corridor in his head stopped insisting on a trophy and started looking like a path that came back to the same sink with slightly cleaner hands.
