# Chapter 2
## The Argument With Nothing

By mid-morning the tea had been replaced twice and the notebook page still said **Start here**, which was beginning to feel less like a vow and more like a dare.

Michael tried to invent nothing.

This is harder than it sounds. Most people who claim to believe in nothing still believe in breakfast, bus times, and the moral necessity of their own opinions. Nothing, as a lifestyle, tends to arrive with a lot of stuff—podcasts, black jumpers, a carefully maintained contempt for other people’s hobbies.

He cleared a kitchen chair. In his head he named it the Chair of Non-Being, which was already cheating, because names are furniture. Even the word *nothing* is a something you can shout across a room and have someone answer *what?*

‘Right,’ he told the empty seat. ‘You’re not there.’

The chair continued its policy of being the sort of object you could bark your shin on. It did not shimmer. It did not apologise. It sat there with the serene insolence of wood that has never read a philosophy book and never needed to.

His phone rang. Joshua had a gift for calling at the exact moment Michael was losing an argument with an inanimate object.

‘You’re arguing with furniture again,’ Joshua said, without preamble.

‘I’m arguing with pure absence as a coherent alternative to existence.’

‘Same hobby,’ Joshua said. ‘Different hat. Have you eaten?’

‘Tea.’

‘That’s not a food group, Michael. That’s a postponement strategy.’

Michael paced the length of the kitchen, which was not long enough for a proper philosophical march and so forced him into a kind of ontological shuffle.

‘If nothing were a real rival to everything,’ he said, ‘it would need to be a place, or a wall, or a before. Places exist. Walls exist. Befores are stories, and stories happen to someone. You can’t clock nothing in for a shift without borrowing existence’s uniform.’

‘There it is,’ Joshua murmured, as if watching a goal from a difficult angle. ‘Non-existence can’t sponsor openings. Or endings. Or borders. It can’t even sponsor the word *or*.’

‘Exactly. The moment you put nothing on the menu, you’ve already hired a waiter, printed the menu, and invented hunger.’

Joshua laughed softly. ‘You’re going to get yourself banned from polite dinner parties.’

‘Good. Polite dinner parties are where people pretend the soup is the main event.’

The kettle clicked off, smugly existent. Steam did its little ghost impression and joined the air, which had never been nothing either. Michael poured water onto a third teabag with the grim determination of a man building a fort out of mugs.

He sat in the Chair of Non-Being.

It immediately became a chair of being-with-buttocks.

The victory was ridiculous and absolute. You could not *experience* nothing without turning it into experience. You could not *think* nothing without thinking. You could not *border* the world with pure not-is without putting the border on the payroll of is-ness. Nothing, as a menu alternative to existence, was a grammar error wearing a cloak and hoping nobody asked for its ticket.

‘So existence wins by default,’ Joshua said. ‘Not because it’s pretty. Because the other candidate didn’t show up and also couldn’t have, because showing up is a kind of existing.’

‘It’s worse than that,’ Michael said. He watched a crumb on the table refuse to become metaphysical. ‘Existence isn’t even a candidate. It’s the hall the election is held in.’

There was a pause of the sort brothers use when one of them has said something true and both of them would rather not put a flag on it yet.

Outside, a bus sighed at the stop. A dog barked twice, then apparently lost interest in the metaphysical project. Inside, something quiet and heavy settled into place—not a belief, not a slogan. More like a brick someone had finally admitted was load-bearing.

Existence was not one option among others. It was the only kitchen. The menu, such as it was, was internal: this tea or that tea, this argument or that silence, this day or the next—but always *here*, always *is*, always the same stubborn fact that had woken him that morning and refused to be unplugged.

‘Do the washing-up,’ Joshua said. ‘Horizontal work. Stops the brain floating off like a balloon with a philosophy degree.’

‘I was going to open every door in the flat,’ Michael admitted.

‘Of course you were. Washing-up first. Then doors. Then ring me back if the universe has a tradesman’s entrance.’

Michael washed a mug. Ontology, he was learning, survived chores. It had to. Otherwise it was only a hobby for men with clean kitchens and no real week ahead of them, and he intended—at least for this week—to have neither excuse.

He dried his hands on a tea towel that had seen better decades, looked at the door to the hall, and felt the next suspicion forming, as clean and ordinary as the cold floor under his socks.

If pure nothing could not sit outside existence like a rival kingdom…

…then what, exactly, were doors for?
