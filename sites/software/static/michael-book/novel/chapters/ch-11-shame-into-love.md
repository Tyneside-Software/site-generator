# Chapter 11
## Shame Into Love

Joshua’s kitchen on Thursday night was smaller than Michael’s and more honest about it. Magnets held takeaway menus, a postcard from somewhere with too much sky, and the Both/And beer mat, already soft at one corner from fridge condensation.

‘Apron,’ Joshua said, throwing him a tea towel. ‘You wash. I dry. No metaphysics until the cutlery sings.’

Michael washed. Hot water, cheap lemon soap, the domestic squeak of plates becoming innocent again. It was hard to be grand with foam on your wrists. That was the point. He scrubbed a baking tray that had known sausages and, briefly, fire, until the black bits surrendered. Joshua dried with the efficient flick of someone who has lived alone long enough to respect a clean rack.

They worked through the stack: pans that had known tomatoes, glasses with fingerprint histories, a single stubborn mug that had seen things. The radio muttered a phone-in about traffic. A caller was furious about temporary lights on the ring road; the presenter made sympathetic noises that cost nothing. Rain ticked the window in a rhythm too irregular to be symbolic and too steady to ignore.

Michael almost started a sentence about Rome and stopped himself, hands still in the water. Joshua noticed and said nothing, which was its own kind of applause.

When the last fork was away, Joshua poured tea without asking and sat on the counter like a teenager who had never left and a man who had come back on purpose.

‘Last night you said *do not let this stay merely clever*,’ he said. ‘Good line. Now keep it.’

Michael leaned against the sink. ‘I keep almost turning the cross into a party trick. Look, geometry! Look, history! Watch me join Rome to a plus sign and a bus poster. It plays well. It would get nods in a pub. And every time I do it in my head I feel the shame bit slide off—the bit that makes the symbol expensive—and what’s left is a diagram wearing a costume.’

Joshua nodded. ‘Shame into love is not a rebrand exercise. You don’t take the humiliation off the wood with a clever wipe. The claim—if you let the claim near you—is that love went *into* the shame and didn’t treat it as a stain to be managed by PR. That’s harder. It doesn’t fit on a mat.’

‘Can it fit in a kitchen?’

‘If it can’t, burn the theology books and keep the soap. I’m serious. If your big shapes don’t survive ordinary repair work—dishes, apologies, showing up when you’d rather be impressive—then they’re hobbies. Hobbies are fine. Don’t call them the axis of the world.’

Michael looked at his hands. Clean now. Still the same hands that had typed, held, mistaken. ‘I don’t want a hobby. I also don’t want a club. Clubs are either/or with membership fees.’

‘Then take the middle path that isn’t mush. Let the structure teach you how to stay. Vertical: something about the whole that isn’t just your mood. Horizontal: the person in front of you, including me when I’m annoying, including you when you’re tired and not done. Join: the cost of not dropping either beam when holding both hurts.’

The fridge clicked. The beer mat gleamed faintly under the kitchen bulb.

Michael said, quieter, ‘When I was younger I thought love was a feeling you waited to be ambushed by. Then I thought it was a moral scoreboard. Now I wonder if it’s more like refusing to let shame have the last word without pretending shame was never in the room.’

Joshua’s eyes were bright. Not tears, exactly. Attention. ‘There. That’s not clever. That’s adult. Keep speaking like that and the diagrams can come back as servants instead of masters.’

They took the tea to the small sofa. A film neither of them cared about played with the sound low, a moving wallpaper of car chases in a city that was not theirs. Someone on screen exploded a car for reasons the plot had not earned; Joshua snorted into his mug. Michael found he could sit without pacing. The week’s bricks were still stacked in him—experience, no pure nothing, one room, no edge, museum, both/and, delimiters, crossbeams, plus signs—but for once he was not trying to build a cathedral before bedtime. The sofa accepted the weight of an unfinished man without filing a complaint.

‘Thank you for not letting me get away with smart,’ he said.

‘Someone has to. The world is full of smart that never did the washing-up. It writes books and abandons people. I’m not interested in you becoming that. You’re allowed to be brilliant. You’re not allowed to be brilliant *instead*.’

Michael laughed into his mug. ‘You’d make a terrible cult leader.’

‘I’d make an excellent one. That’s why I wash dishes. Countermeasure.’

Later, walking home under streetlights that made the wet road look varnished, Michael felt the difference in his body: less spark, more weight. The good kind. The kind that means a thought has agreed to live downstairs with the laundry and the bills and the people. A fox crossed the road ahead of him with the unhurried confidence of a creature that has never needed a theory of the whole, and Michael found himself wishing it well, which was ridiculous and also fine.

From the hall of his own flat he texted: **Cross not allowed to stay clever. Logged.**

Joshua: **Good. Sleep. Tomorrow the world gets wider than our two beams. Be careful and kind.**

Michael set the phone face down. He hung his coat. He filled a glass of water and left it by the bed like a small treaty with morning.

In the dark before sleep he did not diagram anything. He only noticed that shame and love were both still in the room with him—not resolved, not cancelled—

—fastened, for now, at a quiet join he did not yet have a better word for than *stay*.
