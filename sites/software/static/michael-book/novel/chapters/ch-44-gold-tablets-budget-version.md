# Chapter 44
## Gold Tablets (Budget Version)

“I keep wanting to bury it,” Michael said.

Joshua’s kitchen smelled of toast and the particular cleanliness of a house that had decided to be kind to its surfaces. Rain argued with the window. Windows are patient. On the counter a butter knife lay sticky with honesty.

Joshua buttered toast with judicial calm. “Bury what?”

“The chain. The noticings. The no-outside thing. All of it.” Michael turned the plate without eating. “Put it in a box. Put the box in the ground. Invent a respectable story about angels and metal. Come back later when I’m a different man with better lighting.”

Joshua handed him toast. “Gold tablets, budget version.”

“Exactly. I can’t afford gold. I can afford a biscuit tin and a shovel and a myth about why the truth had to hide.”

They both knew the joke’s pedigree. Sacred history is full of finds, digs, plates, stones that wait under dirt until someone is ready—or claims to be. Michael was not mocking the hungry human need for a sealed deposit of meaning. He was confessing the temptation to outsource courage to archaeology: let the earth hold what his nerves would not.

“If it’s buried,” he said, “I don’t have to live it in traffic. I can be the discoverer later. Discoverers get documentaries. People who just wash plates with a new ontology get silence and crumbs.”

Joshua chewed, thought, swallowed. Crumbs fell onto his cuff; he brushed them off as if evidence required tidy sleeves. “Two problems. One: you’re already living it. The burial would be theatre. Two: gold-tablet stories often come with control systems—who may read, who may not, who holds the seer stone. You’re trying to escape a landfill for sin and accidentally building a locked cabinet for insight.”

Michael winced. “That’s uncomfortably neat.”

“I’m your brother. Neat is free.”

They ate. Outside, a car went through a puddle with the confidence of a creature that believes splashes are other people’s problem.

After lunch they walked in the rain with hoods up, looking like conspirators who had forgotten the conspiracy. A churchyard offered a shortcut. Wet stone. Names half-erased. A plastic flower in a jam jar leaned as if listening. The dead did not hand over tablets. They handed over the quieter fact of having been here, of rain knowing how to find carved letters.

Michael stopped at a slab he could not read. “I don’t need metal. I need permission to keep the draft aboveground where it can be wrong in public.”

“Permission from whom?” Joshua asked.

Michael almost said God, then almost said Cameron, then heard himself properly—the correction landing before the overshoot finished. “From the part of me that thinks unfinished truth is shameful.”

Joshua kicked a pebble along the path. It skittered, found a puddle, drowned with dignity. “Unfinished is the only honest format for a living claim. Gold is for endings you want to freeze. You’re not finished. Neither is the field.”

They stopped under a yew that had seen worse weather and worse theologies. Water tapped Michael’s shoulder like a reminder with no doctrine attached. He imagined the biscuit tin anyway—notebook pages, a cheap USB stick, a note saying *for later*—and then rain finding the tin, rust negotiating with revelation, a fox digging for something more edible. Comedy saved him. The budget version of sacred burial was just anxiety in a costume with a shovel accessory.

“I’ll keep it on the desk,” he said. “No dig. No angelic logistics. If it’s true, it can survive being seen by the cat.”

“The cat is an excellent peer reviewer,” Joshua said. “Unimpressed by everything.”

They walked on. At the gate a woman with a collie nodded as if the three of them were in the same weather, which they were. Michael felt the old urge rise—seal it, hide it, become the man who found it later with clean hands—and let it pass. His fingers unclenched inside his pocket. Courage, for once, was not a speech. It was a hand.

That evening he cleared a corner of the desk properly. Not shrine. Not archive. Space. He put the notebook there, open to the sin lines and the plank line and the appointed-hours rule. No lid. No lock. A biro rolled against the spine and stayed, a small secular angel with a chewed cap.

He wrote three lines and stopped before the fourth became a sermon:

**Gold tablets (budget version): refused.**  
**Delivery method: ordinary air.**  
**Storage: aboveground, correctable, slightly embarrassed.**

The phone buzzed once. Joshua: *Still want a shovel?*

Michael typed with butter still faintly on his thumb: *Only for the garden. Beans, not scripture.*

That was the one exchange. Enough.

He turned off the lamp. The notebook stayed visible in the dark like a small pale rectangle of unfinished nerve. From the hall the cat arrived, jumped, settled on the open page with the absolute authority of creatures who do not care about ontology. Ink under fur. Michael scratched behind the ear; the cat forgave nothing and approved nothing and stayed.

No angel arrived to congratulate him.

Something better happened: he felt the relief of not having to maintain a secret archaeology department in his own skull. No midnight dig. No inventory of who was pure enough to read. No future self inventing provenance for courage postponed until the lighting improved.

The truth, if it was a truth, could live where toast crumbs lived.

Outside, rain kept arguing with glass. Inside, the draft stayed aboveground, wrong-capable, public to at least one unimpressed mammal—and that was not less sacred than metal in dirt.

That was the whole point of refusing the dig.
