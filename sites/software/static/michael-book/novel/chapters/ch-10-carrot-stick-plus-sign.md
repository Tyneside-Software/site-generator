# Chapter 10
## Carrot, Stick, Plus Sign

Thursday morning the bus was late, which gave the universe a chance to be funny in public.

Michael stood in the queue with office faces and school bags and a man eating a pastry with the concentration of a monk. On the shelter wall someone had drawn a crude plus sign in marker pen and, underneath, the words **BE KIND** in a handwriting that suggested either a child or an adult reclaiming childhood on purpose.

He thought of carrots and sticks.

Not the vegetables. The old management cartoon: dangled reward, threatened pain, the animal of the self trotting between them as if meaning were a commute. He had lived whole seasons that way—project carrot, deadline stick, praise carrot, shame stick—until the road itself disappeared and only the prodding remained.

Joshua’s string cross from last night sat in his mind’s eye, still humble on the table.

Carrot. Stick. Two poles. A fork for livestock.

Plus sign. Two strokes that *join*.

The bus arrived in a sigh of brakes. Michael tagged on and found a seat by the window. Rain-smear turned the street into an impressionist painting of wheelie bins.

He texted Joshua: **Path: carrot/stick → + → cross?**

Joshua, who kept hours that suggested either holiness or unemployment (he had a job; the holiness was optional), replied within a minute: **Go on. Use your words. Bus brains are allowed to be drafty.**

Michael typed with one thumb, the draft arriving in pieces: carrot and stick as either/or engines—move toward, move away—useful for training dogs and bad cultures; plus sign as the same geometry as a cross without the history—join, add, relate; cross as plus sign that went through Rome and came back carrying weight. Maybe incentives were only the baby version of a deeper structure: not just push-pull, but fastening.

Joshua: **If you laugh on the bus I’ll know you’ve seen it.**

Michael did laugh—once, sharp, the kind that makes the person opposite glance up and then politely invent a reason to look at their phone. Because it *was* funny, in the cosmic sense: the whole moral technology of reward and punishment strutting about as if it owned the road, while underneath it a quieter shape kept saying *and*. Not *or*. *And*.

Addition, not mere herding.

He watched a parent further down the aisle negotiate a toddler’s meltdown with a biscuit (carrot) and a firm *no* (stick) and then, when both failed, simply hold the child until the weather passed—something else: presence, fastening, the horizontal beam in miniature.

The bus took a corner. Michael’s bag leaned into his leg. Everything continued to fit, annoyingly.

At his stop he got off into air that smelled of wet stone. The office building accepted him with its usual glass indifference. In the lift he saw his own face reflected: a man who had begun the week trying not to exist hard enough to unplug experience, and was now giggling about geometry on public transport. Progress, of a sort.

Lunchtime he met Joshua in the little park between the office blocks—two benches, one optimistic tree, a flock of pigeons running a protection racket for crumbs.

‘Carrot and stick,’ Joshua said, stealing a crisp from Michael’s packet, ‘keep you moving. They don’t tell you what the moving is *for*. Plus says: these belong together. Cross says: belonging costs, and the cost was not theoretical.’

‘I don’t want to baptise every corporate plus icon. I’ve sat through too many slide decks where a plus sign meant *synergy* and synergy meant *redundancies with better fonts*.’

‘Then don’t. Keep the distinction. Baby geometry. Grown history. The joke is only load-bearing if you refuse to let marketing steal it.’

Michael leaned back on the bench. A cyclist went past with a small flag of mud. ‘The laugh on the bus was relief. I’ve been trying to climb out of push-pull ethics—be good or get hit, be good or miss the treat—and the week keeps handing me joins instead. Experience *and* thought. Sacred *and* ordinary. Vertical *and* horizontal. Even the old data file: field *and* field *and* field.’

‘Both/And franchise expanding. Next you’ll be putting plus signs on the beer mats.’

‘Only if we can ban the word synergy.’

‘Banned. Out of stock with superiority.’

They sat until the hour threatened employment consequences, then stood. Joshua punched Michael’s shoulder lightly, a horizontal sacrament.

‘Dishes tonight. My place. Don’t be clever about the cross until you’ve held a plate. Clever is cheap. Soap is true.’

Michael walked back to the glass building with the plus sign from the bus shelter still glowing in memory—crude marker, kind instruction, baby geometry on a wet wall. In the lobby a motivational poster offered a ladder and a mountain and the usual lie that the view was only for people who climbed alone. He almost laughed again and did not, because twice in one morning would have been greedy.

At his desk he opened the morning’s tasks as if they were small animals that needed feeding rather than weapons aimed at his self-worth. He answered three emails without using the word *synergy*, which felt like a tiny moral victory of the kind that does not get medals and does not need them. A fourth email nearly dragged him into a carrot-and-stick spiral—praise dangled, deadline implied—and he replied with a clear *yes*, a clear *when*, and nothing that treated himself like livestock.

Somewhere under the carrots and sticks of the working day a quieter arithmetic continued: not merely toward and away, but *with*.
