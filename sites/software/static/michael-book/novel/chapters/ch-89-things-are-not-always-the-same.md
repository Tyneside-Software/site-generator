# Chapter 89
## Things Are Not Always The Same

Unity had a hangover risk: the urge to say *same* until difference died of smothering.

Michael noticed it mid-sentence on a work call, which is a rude place for theology to ambush you. A colleague was struggling—voice flat, tired, the particular fatigue of people who have been told to be resilient as if resilience were a free app. Michael had been about to say *we’re all in the same boat* when the colleague said, without drama:

‘Actually, my boat has a hole and yours has a motor, so please don’t.’

Silence on the line. Office noise in the background like a sea that had not chosen a side. Somewhere a printer chewed paper with the commitment of a small animal.

Michael shut up. He listened. He took notes that were practical—deadlines that could move, a contact in HR, an offer to cover Thursday’s meeting. He helped without poetry. After the call he walked the block swearing softly at his own universal solvent, the way it dissolved other people’s specifics into a warm bath called Oneness and congratulated itself for being kind.

He called the emergency council: Joshua in person on a park bench that had lost a slat, R. on speakerphone, the wind doing its best to edit everyone.

‘I’ve been drunk on one field,’ Michael said. He sat on the good side of the bench, the side that still believed in backs. ‘No outside is true for me. Then I treat pain as interchangeable. Things are not always the same. Parallel books, not identical books. Different holes. Different motors.’

R.’s voice came clear over the wind. ‘Thank you. When people say “same” too fast, my history disappears. So does disability. So does money. Unity that erases is just empire with yoga pants.’

Joshua kicked a pebble into a puddle. The puddle accepted it without a sermon. ‘Both/and includes *and different*. The cross had two beams, not one soup. Horizontal means neighbour as neighbour—not as your mirror. If Denise’s boiler and your monogram are “the same,” you’ve lost the plot and the wrench.’

Michael laughed without fun. ‘I almost baptised a hole into a metaphor to make myself comfortable. Comfort is a bad priest.’

They worked examples like first aid, Joshua counting on fingers:

‘Same: both need respect. Not the same: who gets stopped by security.’

‘Same: both can be wrong. Not the same: whose wrong gets a newspaper.’

‘Same: both die eventually. Not the same: who dies early from policy.’

R. added, quieter, ‘Your monogram can stay generative without saying every body is the same body. Generation itself depends on difference. Ask a biologist. Ask anyone who has ever needed a particular medicine. Ask me when you almost made my seal your open page’s twin.’

Michael looked at his hands—ordinary, uninjured, free to type, free to walk a block while angry at himself, free to leave a meeting early because no one would dock his dignity for it. The wind found the gap in his jacket and made its case.

‘I’ll practise *not the same* as a holy phrase this week,’ he said. ‘Alongside *one field*. If that tensions, good. Tension means I haven’t flattened the map into a poster next to the fridge magnets of enlightenment.’

Joshua slung an arm around his shoulder. The bench creaked in solidarity. ‘Welcome back to edges inside the whole. Edges aren’t outsides. They’re how shapes work. How boats float. How jam jars have labels so you don’t spread apricot on a wound that needed something else.’

That evening Michael sat at the kitchen table and forced a notebook column headed *NOT THE SAME* with concrete names—not abstractions, not fields, not the soft blur of cosmic goodwill. People he loved. People he had failed. Systems with teeth. The colleague’s boat, as named by the colleague, not as rebranded by Michael’s need to feel spacious.

Harriet came in, hung her bag, glanced at the page without reading it like a spy.

‘You look like a man who has been told off by reality,’ she said.

‘I have. Reality was right.’ He closed the notebook. ‘Tea?’

‘Always. Don’t explain the ontology of kettles.’

He did not. He made tea for two, handed her the mug with the chip she preferred because it fitted her hand, and only later messaged the colleague: *You were right about the boats. Motor is yours to name. Help is mine to offer without poetry.*

The reply was brief and kind: *Thanks. Poetry later maybe. Today just the hole.*

Michael put the phone face down. Outside, a neighbour dragged a bin that squeaked like an argument settling. Inside, Harriet told him about a difficult email and a kind stranger at the chemist, and he asked follow-up questions that had nothing to do with unity and everything to do with her day.

Things are not always the same.

The recognition did not die. It grew a spine, and the spine had room for particular leaks, particular motors, particular faces that refused to be melted into a warm bath—

—and love, for once, looked less like blending and more like showing up with the right tool for a hole that was not a metaphor.
