# Chapter 42
## Verse Forty-Two

The chapter number arrived like a prop that had been waiting in the wings.

M. noticed it on the notebook spine he had labelled in a fit of organisational optimism—*Draft 42*—and laughed with the particular embarrassment of a man caught by his own joke. Forty-two had already done duty as ordinary answer, towel philosophy, C.’s seatbelt. Luke had already loaned a plank. Now the calendar of the book itself was winking.

“Very funny,” he told the ceiling, which declined to take credit.

He made tea and opened the verse properly this time—not as a weapon for other people’s eyes, not as a badge of humility, but as a mirror he could not hire someone else to hold.

*How can you say to your brother, “Brother, let me take the speck out of your eye,” when you yourself fail to see the plank in your own eye?*

The comedy was structural. Carpentry props. Surgical pretence. A man offering optometry while wearing a forest.

J. came mid-morning with pastries that were slightly crushed and therefore honest. Butter had migrated into the paper bag; the bag looked like a crime scene involving croissants.

“Verse forty-two,” M. said, and slid the notebook across.

J. read, then looked at the chapter label, then grinned. “The book is bullying you.”

“Deserved.” M. tore a pastry; flakes snowed onto the table like a small confetti of accountability. “I spent yesterday dismantling sin-as-elsewhere and nearly built a new superiority out of the rubble. *Look how carefully I refuse exile.* That’s a plank with better manners.”

“Planks love manners,” J. said. “Manners make them harder to see.”

Outside, a neighbour argued with a recycling bin about its destiny—plastic in the wrong mouth, lid slamming, the full opera of domestic judgment. Ordinary speck-tourism, live demo, no tickets required.

“C.’s forty-two and Luke’s forty-two keep shaking hands in my head,” M. said. “Adams: deflate the cosmic swagger. Luke: deflate the moral swagger. Same department, different uniforms.”

“And the chapter number?” J. asked.

“A reminder not to write a book that removes everyone else’s speck while I narrate my own enlightenment in soft lighting.”

J. raised his mug. “Amen, you forest.”

After J. left, M. did the least glamorous spiritual practice available. He sat with a pen and listed three people he had recently corrected without first asking whether he was hungry, tired, or performing wisdom.

One name got an apology text that he actually sent—short, specific, no theology attached.

One name stayed in drafts because timing was also ethics; a true apology at the wrong hour is just another plank wearing perfume.

One name needed a walk, not a sermon. He put on a coat and went.

On the path by the field the spaniel from the alpha week was winning again—sniffing, existing, ignoring a man who still believed dominance was a personality. M. felt the itch to educate the owner about pack myths. The itch was a speck that thought it was a calling. He kept walking. The spaniel did not require his thesis. The field smelled of wet grass and someone else’s dog.

At a café he overheard two strangers dissect a third who was not present. The dissection was skilled, even entertaining—cadence, timing, the pleasure of being on the clean side of a fence. He recognised the pleasure in his own mouth like a flavour. Yesterday’s chapter had tried to abolish the fence as ontology. Today’s verse abolished it as moral tourism.

He almost messaged C. a sermonette titled *I Am The Plank Exhibit*. He deleted it. Public plank-lists, he suspected J. would say, are reverse vanity—humility as content.

Instead he rang C. while walking home, wind in the microphone.

“If you’re about to preach Luke at people who need lunch,” C. said without hello, “I’m reinstating the towel ban.”

“Lunch first,” M. said. “Optometry second. Also: the book numbered this chapter forty-two and I am choosing not to found a religion about that.”

“Good,” C. said. “Religions founded on chapter numbers have worse hymns than digital clocks. You’re learning. Slowly. Like a forest.”

Evening: crumbs still on the table, verse still open, ordinary number still ordinary. C.’s forty-two still protected people from intensity cults. Luke’s forty-two still protected people from hypocrisy with a toolkit. The book’s forty-two was just a wink: *you are not above the joke you are telling.*

M. washed two mugs until they squeaked. Discipleship that cannot manage crockery is mostly press release.

He left the verse open on the table on purpose—not as décor, as a friend who refused to flatter.

Some mirrors work better if you do not put them away too quickly.
