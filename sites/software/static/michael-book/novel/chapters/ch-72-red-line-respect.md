# Chapter 72
## Red Line Respect

Thursday they met early, before Scripture Reasoning, in the little park with the optimistic tree that still occasionally wore plastic like a party that had forgotten to end.

R. arrived with two flat whites and a face that said *business first, then friendship*, which is sometimes the only order that saves both from drowning in manners.

‘I want a red line said out loud,’ she said. ‘Not implied. Not polite fog. Fog is how people get hurt while everyone claims they meant well.’

Michael sat on the bench. The wood was cold through his coat. ‘Say it. I’ll repeat it back until you believe I heard—not until I can weaponise a summary.’

R. counted on her fingers, not theatrical—practical, like packing a bag for a journey that might include weather.

‘One: no conversion project. Not soft, not clever, not “I just want you to be free.” I am not a trophy for your monogram. I am not the prize at the end of your both/and.’

‘One: no conversion project,’ Michael said. ‘You are not a trophy. If I slide, you say *trophy* and I stop. No appeal, no rebranding the slide as love.’

‘Two: no explaining Islam to me as if I were a tourist in my own house. Questions allowed. Lectures banned. Curiosity that talks more than it listens is still a lecture in a nice jumper.’

‘Two: questions, not lectures. If I lecture, you walk. I don’t get to call the walking unkind.’

‘Three: my seal stays mine. You can dislike final-chapter language; you cannot rebrand it as your both/and accessory. My completion is not your missing piece.’

‘Three: seal stays yours. I don’t rebrand it. I don’t wear it as proof that my system already ate the world.’

‘Four: if we disagree in public or in a group, you don’t use our friendship as evidence that your system already includes me. Friendship is not a footnote. I am not a citation.’

Michael swallowed. That one had been close enough to taste, once—cleverness dressing itself as inclusion. ‘Four: friendship is not evidence for my thesis. It’s a relationship. Full stop. If I need a thesis, I write one that doesn’t draft you without consent.’

R. watched his face for the micro-expressions of men who agree in order to win later. She found effort instead—uglier, better, the look of someone doing work rather than performing sincerity.

‘Five,’ she said more quietly. ‘If your recognition makes you kinder, bring the kindness. If it makes you hungry to be right about me, leave. I will not audit your soul. I will leave a room. Leaving is not a tantrum. It is a boundary with legs.’

‘Five: kindness without capture. Leave if I hunt. I will treat your leaving as data, not as persecution.’

She nodded once. ‘Your turn. Your red lines. Don’t pretend you haven’t got any. People who claim to have no red lines usually have invisible ones and trip you with them.’

He hadn’t expected the reciprocity so cleanly. It steadied him—the mutuality of fences, not the loneliness of being the only one asked to behave.

‘One: don’t treat my monogram as only a sex joke or only blasphemy without hearing the chain that got me there. Mock the excess. Don’t erase the work. I can take sandpaper. I can’t take being reduced to a punchline so you don’t have to think.’

‘Heard,’ R. said. No flinch. Good.

‘Two: Cameron-style sandpaper is welcome; contempt is not. I will take *you’re doing the cardigan again*. I won’t take *you’re disposable*. Disagreement isn’t exile.’

‘Fair. Contempt is laziness with a sneer. We can do better.’

‘Three: Joshua is not a weapon for either of us. No *your brother agrees with me* as a club. He’s a person, not a swing vote.’

R. almost smiled. ‘Agreed. He’s too useful as a human to be demoted to ammunition.’

‘Four: I get to keep writing the bigger book without that being framed as an attack on you by default. Critique the content. Don’t pathologise the desire to think. Hunger isn’t automatically imperialism—though imperialism loves to wear hunger’s coat.’

She considered, head tilted, the park’s thin sunlight catching a thread on her scarf. ‘Four, with an amendment: you get the desire. You don’t get immunity when the thinking steps on me. You write; I push back; neither of us pretends pushback is betrayal. Deal?’

‘Deal.’

They sat with the list between them like a third person who would keep the minutes and refuse to be romanticised. A toddler chased a pigeon with the eternal optimism of toddlers. The pigeon, a veteran of parks and false hopes, left with professional dignity.

‘People think respect is soft,’ R. said. ‘Respect is a fence with a gate. Fence so we don’t trample. Gate so we can still visit. No fence is not love. No fence is a free-for-all where the strongest feelings win.’

‘Pratchett would like that,’ Michael said, then added, ‘Sorry—habit of citing imaginary allies when a living one just said something better.’

‘Cite whoever helps you behave,’ R. said. ‘Just don’t cite me into your footnotes as *the Muslim friend who proves universality*. I am not a proof. I am a person who drinks coffee and has red lines and will not be your universality’s poster.’

He put a hand over his heart, half comic, half vow—the sort of gesture that only works if the next months make it true. ‘Red line respect. Written where I can’t pretend I didn’t hear it.’

She clinked her cup against his. Cardboard against cardboard; no ceremony; better than ceremony. ‘Good. Now we can be friends on purpose, not by accident of manners. Manners are cheap. Purpose costs.’

They walked toward the centre together, not in step—people rarely are—and the red lines walked with them, invisible, load-bearing, kinder than the fog that pretends love needs no borders and then is surprised when someone gets hurt in the mist.

A bus sighed at the lights. A shop was already selling flowers that would outlast most arguments. Inside, soon, the circle would open again—texts, voices, the careful sport of not winning.

Outside, for a few more minutes, two people practised the rare adult skill of saying what must not be done, and meaning it enough to stay.

At the door of the building R. paused. ‘If you forget, I’ll remind you. If I forget, you may remind me. No scoreboard.’

‘No scoreboard,’ Michael said.

They went in. The flat whites were finished. The red lines were not.
