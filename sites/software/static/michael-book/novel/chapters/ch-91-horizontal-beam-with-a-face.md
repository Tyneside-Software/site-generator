# Chapter 91
## Horizontal Beam With A Face

The neighbour’s name was Denise, and Michael had spent two years calling her *the neighbour* as if anonymity were politeness rather than laziness with better manners.

She knocked because her boiler had chosen a dramatic death on a Thursday evening and she needed a pair of hands and a lift to the hardware place before it closed. Harriet was on a late shift. Michael had been mid-sentence in a note about capstones and peaks and the various ways men climb things that should not be climbed. He said yes before his monogram could hold a meeting about meaning, put shoes on wrong-footed, fixed them at the door while Denise waited with the patience of someone whose heating had already made its point.

In the car she talked about her grandson, her hip, the price of bread, a sister in another city who didn’t call enough. The heater smelled of dust burning off. Michael listened. When he spoke, he asked about the grandson’s school play—sheep, apparently, with a speaking line and a costume that itched. He did not mention fonts. He did not mention God except as the kind of weather people still thank without paperwork, the way you thank a bus that arrives when you have given up.

At the store the fluorescent lights made everyone look slightly guilty, as if shopping for parts were a minor crime. They bought the piece the internet swore was right, plus washers the man at the counter insisted on with the authority of experience and a life spent watching amateurs invent new ways to leak. Back at Denise’s flat Michael lay on the kitchen floor that smelled of other people’s cooking oil and followed a video with the concentration of prayer. The host said *easy* in the way liars and saints both do. Denise made tea too strong and apologised for the mugs not matching.

‘Matching is empire,’ Michael said, then winced at himself. ‘Sorry. Private joke. Mugs are fine. This one is excellent. It holds tea. That’s the whole exam.’

She laughed without needing the joke explained, which is a gift rarer than matching china. They bled radiators, coaxed valves, swore carefully at a nut that had rusted into personality. Heat returned with a rattle of forgiveness. Denise put her palm near the pipe like checking a child’s forehead, the old human test: is it warm, is it safe, can we stop bracing.

She pressed a tenner on him. He refused. She looked offended in a way that meant dignity, not greed—the right to close a debt so it does not hang between people like a damp towel. He accepted a jar of jam instead. Homemade. Label in biro: *Apricot — D*. Bond as jam. Theology that could go on toast.

On the stair he paused, jam cold against his wrist. Horizontal beam—God and neighbour, the old cross-geometry from the week before monograms got rude—had always risked being a diagram. Denise was not a diagram. She had a face with lines, a voice with a city in it, a boiler with opinions, a sister named Carol who didn’t call enough and probably had reasons that would not fit on a beer mat.

He texted Joshua a photo of the jam. *Horizontal beam has a face. Name: Denise.*

Joshua: *Write the name down. Names kill abstraction. Abstraction is how we love categories and neglect doorbells.*

R., copied in by the messy habit of group threads: *Good. Now help her again when it isn’t photogenic. Photos are for you. Help is for her.*

Cameron: *Also service the friendship before you need a lift yourself. Reciprocity, not hero play. Heroes are exhausting to live next to.*

Michael put the jam on his own shelf like a relic that could be eaten—best kind of relic, the sort that refuses museum glass. Harriet, home late, tasted a spoonful standing in her coat and said, ‘She’s a better theologian than half your notes. Notes don’t heat a flat.’

‘Notes heat a certain kind of vanity,’ Michael said.

‘That too. Wash the spoon.’

That weekend he knocked on Denise’s door with surplus soup, not as payment, as continuation. She invited him in for ten minutes of television gossip—someone on a soap had betrayed someone; the audience was supposed to be shocked; Denise was not. He learned Carol’s name properly, and that the grandson had remembered his line after all. He still did not mention α==ω. The boiler hummed like a contented appliance. Praise be to humming. Praise be to parts that fit.

Walking back down the stairwell that always smelled faintly of other dinners, he understood something filmable and small: the beam becomes real when it risks irritation, time loss, incompetence with tools, the smell of someone else’s life, the awkwardness of accepting jam when you wanted to be noble for free. Abstract love of Neighbour is cheap. Love of Denise costs an evening and grants jam and the right to be asked again without a thesis attached.

He added Denise to the ordinary list in his phone under *people*, not *symbols*, and set a reminder for nothing mystical—just *check in mid-month*, the sort of calendar entry that keeps geometry honest.

Night settled. Somewhere a boiler kept faith with its repaired part. Michael washed his hands of metal grit at his own sink and thought of crosses that were death and shame and love—and of how none of that geometry mattered if it never learned a doorbell, a name, a floor that was not his.

Harriet, already half asleep on the sofa, mumbled, ‘You smell of radiators.’

‘Horizontal beam,’ he said.

‘If that’s code for jam, leave some for breakfast.’

He left some for breakfast. Horizontal beam with a face. The funny old life, which has always preferred names to diagrams when the chips are down, filed the evening under ordinary proof—no museum required, only a jar on a shelf and a neighbour who was no longer *the neighbour* but Denise, which is how love starts when it is not trying to win.
