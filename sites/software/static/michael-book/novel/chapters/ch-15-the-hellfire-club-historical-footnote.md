# Chapter 15
## The Hellfire Club (Historical Footnote)

Saturday afternoon Joshua arrived with cake in a slightly crushed box and a laptop already warm from research.

‘Hellfire Club,’ he announced, as if naming a pub. ‘Historical footnote. Rakes, robes, caves if you believe the tourist board, pies allegedly named after sacred things by men who thought blasphemy was a personality. Half of it is legend layered on gossip layered on someone trying to sell a book in 1790. The other half is still embarrassing enough to count.’

Michael cleared the table. The string cross was gone; in its place, crumbs and a shared plate. ‘Why are we doing this at the end of the week? As a cautionary tale?’

‘As a mirror with warts. You’ve spent days building something that could, if you got lonely and vain, tip into cosplay. Secret knowledge. Special club. Mock the sacred so you feel tall. Or the opposite error: polish the sacred until it can’t survive a joke. Hellfire is what happens when satire forgets to love the thing it cuts, and seriousness forgets to breathe.’

They read in tandem—proper sources where possible, sceptical notes where the stories smelled of brandy. Eighteenth-century gentlemen playing at damnation between courses. Empire money. Bored privilege inventing a nightlife of inverted pieties. One account mentioned secret names; another denied the club had ever been more than a dinner with costumes; a third insisted on caves and thunder. The details slid about depending on the author; the shape held: mockery as membership badge.

Michael found a reproduction of a period caricature and winced. Men in robes, faces arranged into daring. A table of excess. The artist had not been kind, and still the subjects seemed to be enjoying the unkindness as publicity. ‘They’re not funny. Not now. Maybe not then. It’s all elbowing for status. Look how brave we are, insulting what other people kneel to, while the servants bring more wine.’

‘Satire needs love,’ Joshua said, cutting cake. ‘Or it becomes the sneer it mocks. Love doesn’t mean soft focus. Love means you still want the good of what you’re criticising—or the good of the people tangled in it. Hellfire wanted a thrill and a club. That’s not critique. That’s costume.’

Michael ate cake and thought of the high-street preacher and the loud sceptic, of his own temptation to be clever about crosses and moons and names. ‘I’ve felt the pull. Not robes. The modern kind. Private language. *We who see the joins.* Beer mats as initiation. It’s funny until it isn’t—until someone outside the joke becomes furniture.’

Joshua pushed the laptop aside. ‘So we close Act I with a rule, not a robe. If we gather—brothers, friends, later whoever the story brings—we gather to listen and repair, not to cosplay damnation or enlightenment. No pies named after holy things for the giggle. No secret alphabet that makes outsiders unclean. The Both/And shop stays open late and still doesn’t sell superiority.’

Michael did not open the notebook. The week’s pages were already full enough; another list would have been a kind of robe. Instead he said the policy aloud, testing how it sounded when it had to live in air: mockery without love is empire in a mask; satire’s job is surgery, not trophy-hunting; no damnation cosplay, no enlightenment cosplay; if it isn’t kind under the joke, bin the joke.

Joshua listened, nodded, then drew a small square on a fresh beer mat he had produced from his pocket like a magician with modest powers.

**ACT I — CLOSED**  
**Tools kept. Thrones refused.**  
**Cake mandatory.**

Michael laughed. ‘That’s not how books end chapters.’

‘It’s how *we* end weeks. Books can catch up.’

They sat until the light thickened toward evening. Outside, Saturday did Saturday things: football scores shouted from an open window, shopping bags that had lost their geometry, a distant siren that belonged to someone else’s emergency and still, annoyingly, fitted in the same field. A dog barked once, professionally, then lost interest. Inside, two brothers finished cake and did not found a religion—which, given the historical company they had been keeping, counted as a modest triumph.

‘Next week the brains will try to arrive late and take credit,’ Joshua said, half teasing, half preview. ‘Allegedly. Prediction errors for breakfast. You can be ready without being armoured.’

‘Keep going,’ Michael said, and the words tasted different now—less like a thunderclap, more like a packed lunch.

‘Keep going,’ Joshua agreed. ‘And when you want to be Hellfire-clever, wash something. Horizontal beam. Works on pride like lemon on grease.’

At the door they hugged—brief, British, sufficient. Michael stood alone in the hall afterward and looked at the doors he had opened on Monday. Still internal. Still here. The map still had no edge made of pure nothing. The museum was still open. The shop still refused false dilemmas. The cross still refused to be merely clever. The name still asked its question.

He put the Act I beer mat on his own fridge, opposite the electricity bill.

Sacred and ordinary.

Then he washed the cake plates, dried them, put them away, and turned off the kitchen light with the quiet satisfaction of a man who has finished a true thing without needing a robe for the afterparty.

Somewhere—everywhere—the week completed itself the only way weeks can: by becoming the next morning, still inside the only room, still offering the same unkillable *is*.

Act I closed with a soft snap.

The tools stayed on the table for Act II.

And the last crumb of cake, which had no theology at all, was eaten without residue of shame—which was, as footnotes go, surprisingly good company for the truth.
