# Chapter 95
## Missionary Without Eggshells

‘I don’t want to be a missionary,’ Michael said.

‘Good,’ said R.

‘I also don’t want to walk on eggshells until I become a ghost who never says what he loves.’

‘Also good,’ said R. ‘Harder. Ghosts are popular at parties. They never spill wine on doctrine. Hosts love ghosts. Doctrine does not.’

They were in the bookshop from the old moon-and-star window week—different display now, same bell above the door, same smell of paper and glue and people pretending not to judge each other’s covers. A stack of hardbacks offered *How to Win* in three fonts. Michael refused the omen. Joshua browsed nearby as emotional cover, holding a poetry book he would not buy. Cameron had refused to enter a bookshop on principle of surplus unread piles and sent a text instead: *If you convert anyone in Soft Furnishings I revoke chip privileges.*

R. pulled a chair in the tiny café at the back. The table wobbled; Michael folded a receipt under the leg—practical theology. Steam rose from two cups with the neutrality of good staff who had seen every kind of convert and every kind of coward.

‘Eggshells are what people do when they fear conflict more than falsehood,’ she said. ‘They smile, they dilute, they call it respect. It’s often just anxiety management. Real respect can bear weight. It can hear *Christ == God* and say *not for me* without ending the meal. It can hear my seal and not rebrand it as your open page.’

Michael turned a sugar sachet end over end. ‘And missionary is what people do when they fear difference more than violence. They love you toward their map until you disappear. How do I speak from love without hunting? I can feel the hunt in my throat when I get excited. Excitement is not permission. Excitement thinks it is.’

Joshua dropped into a chair with the poetry book as prop. ‘Consent. Context. Exit ramps. You share because asked or because friendship has earned a turn—not because silence burns your ego. You stop when the other says stop. You don’t punish stop with coldness. Coldness after no is just missionary work with the heater off. I’ve sat in that cold room. It’s not holy. It’s sulking with a halo.’

R. nodded, tearing a corner off a napkin without writing on it—progress. ‘Missionary without eggshells means: clear speech, low coercion, high warmth, no sulk. You may invite. You may not corner. You may care. You may not collect. If you leave a conversation needing a win, you were hunting. If you leave needing to have been kind, you might have been a friend. Friends don’t keep score of souls.’

Michael practised a sentence, quiet enough for the café, firm enough to be real. ‘I think the monogram is true the way jokes are true—recognition, not billyclub. I live under Christ as God. I think you are whole without becoming me. I would like us to keep arguing kindly. Full stop. No metric on your enthusiasm.’

R. cocked her head, testing for hooks. ‘I’d receive that. I would not receive a follow-up that measured my salvation by my smile at your napkin. No follow-up metrics. Bond. And no silent ledger where you feel disappointed that I didn’t lean further. Ledgers are eggshells’ ugly cousin.’

‘No follow-up metrics,’ Michael said. ‘No silent ledger. Bond.’

A bookseller wheeled a trolley past; new hardbacks gleamed like recruits. Michael felt the old urge to put his book among them as weapon and let it pass like a bus he did not need. Joshua returned the poetry book to the shelf with the tenderness of men who know unread piles are also ethics.

Cameron texted again: *If you need to win the silent argument in your head after, you were still hunting. Notice. Then go home. Home is not a mission field. Home is where you put the chips.*

Michael showed them. Joshua said, ‘Sandpaper gospel. Blessed are the unimpressed.’

They left with no new comparative religion purchases—Michael’s choice, a small fast from acquisition. On the pavement a busker sang something almost true. Michael asked, ‘If someone earnest asks me what I believe at a party, eggshells or mission?’

‘Honesty with volume control,’ R. said. ‘Three sentences. Then questions about them. If they lean in, continue. If they talk about football, play football. God is not always the only ball. Sometimes the ball is actual football. Touch grass. Touch the horizontal beam. Touch the crisp bowl. The crisp bowl has converted more people to friendship than most monologues.’

Michael laughed. ‘That’s the most pastoral thing you’ve said.’

‘Don’t make me a pastor in your chain,’ she said, smiling. ‘Make me a friend who tells you when your volume is for you, not for truth. Volume-for-you is eggshells’ loud cousin—still about your comfort. Comfort dressed as courage is still comfort.’

Home, he wrote the rules on paper and stuck them inside the notebook cover where he would see them before he saw his own cleverness:

**Speak clear.**  
**Don’t corner.**  
**No sulk after no.**  
**Warmth ≠ win.**  
**Football allowed.**  
**Three sentences, then questions.**

Harriet read them over his shoulder. ‘Finally. A missionary position I can live with.’

He groaned. She grinned, unrepentant, and kissed the place where sulking lives.

Missionary without eggshells: adult speech in a world that swings between shout and mute. Room for his voice—and room for everyone else’s silence—without calling either a war.

That night, at a party he almost declined, someone asked what he believed. He gave three sentences. They talked about football. He played. Nobody converted. Nobody became a ghost. The crisp bowl emptied honestly, which is more theology than most slogans deliver.
