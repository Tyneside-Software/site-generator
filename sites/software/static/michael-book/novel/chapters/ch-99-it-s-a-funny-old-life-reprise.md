# Chapter 99
## It's A Funny Old Life (Reprise)

Morning arrived properly, with toast smoke and a busker on the corner practising optimism that the tip jar did not yet believe in.

This was not chapter sixty-five. That landing stayed landed: the pub, the empty chair, the napkin, the dual reading, the line spoken as recognition’s first full breath. No one was rewriting the end of the monogram week. The monogram still lived in the wallet, fold-worn, slightly ridiculous, still true.

This was the morning after living it—weeks of bigger book, red lines, boilers, biscuits, seals, soft play, dinner-table scandal, shelves that remained shelves, foxes in hedges, jam that was not a metaphor until someone weak made it one.

Michael buttered toast for Harriet. One edge had caught; he scraped the bitter off without philosophy. She hummed something off-key and stole a corner of his crust with the authority of long affection. Joshua knocked and entered with the spare key and a bag of apples, as if fibre were a sacrament. Cameron arrived ten minutes later, complaining about the hour and staying anyway, shoes off by the door in the old Friday-night way. R. came last—scarf, smile, no trophy light in anyone’s eyes.

They fit around the table because the table had practised. Elbows negotiated. A neighbour’s cat of temporary custody watched from a chair like a minor official on break.

“Status report,” Cameron said, pouring coffee. “Anyone founded a cult?”

“No,” Michael said.

“Annexed a friend?” R. asked lightly.

“No,” Michael said.

“Crowned a keyboard?” Joshua asked.

“No,” Harriet said for him, firm, and passed the butter as if butter were a creed.

Michael laughed, and the laugh had sleep in it, which made it honest. “I am a man who still thinks α==ω is true, Christ == God is true, no-outside is true, and Denise’s jam is also true. I am trying to keep those truths from eating each other.”

R. took an apple, turned it, found a bruise, ate around the bruise without speechifying. “Acceptable status. Friendship continues.”

Joshua rolled an apple to Cameron. “Amen to continues.”

Cameron caught it one-handed. “Forty-two. And washing-up. In that order if the universe forces a choice.”

They ate. They talked about nothing cosmic: a television show that had wasted a good premise; a work irritation with a name; a plan to fix Denise’s shelf next Saturday; a Scripture Reasoning date that was optional and open. The monogram did not get a speaking role. It didn’t need one. It had become furniture of the mind—like a window, not a throne. Light came through. You did not stand on it to address the nation.

When the plates stacked, Michael stood at the sink and felt the cold floor through socks. First experiment, last experiment: he was here. They were here. The room had no pure elsewhere to dump love or harm into. Steam rose from the tap. Someone’s phone buzzed and was ignored—small modern piety.

Harriet leaned on the counter beside him. “You’re quiet.”

“I’m noticing without a press release,” he said. “It turns out that’s harder than revelation.”

“Do it anyway. And pass the sponge.”

R. washed. Joshua dried. Cameron supervised tea-towel technique with the solemnity of a referee. Michael put away. The choreography was clumsy and kind. A glass nearly died; Joshua saved it; Cameron claimed credit; R. rolled her eyes in three religions’ worth of fondness.

At the door, goodbyes stacked like blessings that refused to be formal. Cameron left first—*before anyone sings*. R. squeezed Michael’s arm once: red lines still green. Joshua hugged him with brother-force and left two apples on purpose. Harriet closed the door and rested her forehead against Michael’s for a second of private horizontal beam—no thesis, heat, home.

Alone in the quiet kitchen, Michael opened the wallet, saw the napkin, and left it there. He did not read it aloud. He did not need to. Outside, the busker hit a wrong note and laughed at himself; the street, being a street, forgave him.

He thought of the first cold morning; of Unfaithful at 06:08; of the ridge wind; of a child on his hip; of a boiler returning to life; of R. saying *guest, not ingredient*; of Cameron going to bed; of Joshua saying amen over chips. The chain was not a cage. It was a path walked with company.

Michael washed the last mug, set it down, and let the day take him—emails, love, preventable horrors, tea—with the only line that could carry climax and aftermath without lying about either.

He said it to the window, to the field, to you—not as discovery now, as practice, as weather reported by a man with jam on the shelf and friends who leave early and stay when it counts:

**It's a funny old life.**

The sky did not write it on a cloud. The toast had burned slightly on one edge.

He ate what remained anyway.
