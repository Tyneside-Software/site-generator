# Chapter 78
## Syncretism Without Theft

‘Syncretism,’ Cameron said, writing the word on a napkin in the sticky-menu pub, ‘is either how cultures actually work or a polite word for shoplifting. Guess which one you have to police in yourself.’

Michael eyed the napkin. Napkins had become dangerous stationery. The last one in his wallet still held a private joke with public consequences. ‘I’m listening.’

Joshua stole a chip from Cameron’s plate with brotherly immunity. ‘He’s been trying to hold moon and star and cross and monogram without doing a fridge-magnet altar.’

‘Correct,’ Michael said. ‘Unity without erasure. Both-and with teeth. Then I catch myself thinking *deep down it’s all the same*, which is the softest theft—stealing difference so I can feel cosy. It’s the spiritual equivalent of saying all music is just noise with a beat. Technically a kind of true. Practically a way to stop listening.’

Cameron underlined *theft* hard enough to tear a fibre. ‘There it is. Syncretism without theft means: influences happen; borrowings happen; you credit; you don’t annex; you don’t baptise someone else’s sacrament into your system and call it hospitality.’

R. arrived late, coat damp, eyes bright with residual work stress. She peered at the napkin as if it might bite. ‘If this is about me, I want voting rights. Non-negotiable. I have seen myself used as a spice in other people’s unity recipes.’

‘It’s about him,’ Cameron said. ‘You’re the quality control. Sit. We ordered you something that pretends to be wine.’

They ordered properly when the server circled back. Michael laid out the fear: that living one field made him want a single shelf where every tradition sat as a flavour of his joke. That the monogram’s dual reading had taught him both-and—and both-and could become a blender if unsupervised. Blenders are violent to fruit. Fruit has a right to remain fruit.

R. took the pen and wrote, in a hand that did not apologise for clarity:

**Guest ≠ ingredient.**

‘You can be changed by Islam without making Islam a chapter of Michaelism,’ she said. ‘You can honour a practice without sandwiching it between your alpha and omega like a press. Syncretism that happens in history is messy and mutual and often unjust. Syncretism you *perform* on purpose from a sofa needs ethics. Sofa syncretism is how people get tattoos of languages they can’t read.’

Joshua nodded, chewing. ‘Credit. Consent where consent applies. Distance where distance is respect. And no “ancient aliens” energy applied to living friends. Ancient aliens energy is just theft with a documentary voiceover.’

Michael laughed, pained. ‘I have felt one per cent ancient aliens energy. I repent.’

‘Repent specifically,’ Cameron said. ‘Name a theft you’ve already done. Out loud. We are not priests; we are the unfortunate friends.’

Michael stared at the table’s scars—decades of glasses, keys, elbows, people who had argued and stayed. ‘I almost used keys six and eight as a moon-star proof. I almost used R. as evidence that my field includes Islam because I have a Muslim friend. I almost turned *will you not think* into a quote that made me look broad-minded in my own diary. I almost told a dinner table that all final chapters are the same fear. They aren’t.’

R. raised her glass an inch. ‘Thank you. Those last ones are subtle. Subtle is where the good shoes of theft live.’

They spent the next hour doing something unfashionable: drawing lines around what Michael would *not* combine. No hybrid ritual invented to show off. No claiming Sufism because he liked poetry. No wearing symbols as fashion. Yes to learning. Yes to being corrected. Yes to letting metaphors rhyme if they rhyme in public, not only in his thrill.

Joshua wrote the list on his phone and emailed it to Michael with the subject line: *DO NOT BECOME A BLENDER*. Michael’s phone buzzed. He starred it, which is a small liturgy of intention.

‘Syncretism without theft looks boring from the outside,’ Joshua said. ‘Fewer tattoos. More listening. Possibly better. Possibly you die with an unimpressive Instagram. Survive that.’

When they parted, Cameron left the napkin. Michael almost kept it, then binned it on purpose—another napkin retired before it became scripture. The bin swung shut with a soft plastic amen.

Walking home in damp air, he felt poorer and cleaner. A bus threw light across shop windows full of mass-produced ‘spiritual’ décor: crystals, slogans, symbols freelancing without context. He did not go in.

The bigger book could still have many inks.

It could not, if he was serious about red lines, be written in stolen colours and called light—and the honest dullness of not taking what wasn’t offered felt, for once, like a brightness that did not need a shop window to prove it was shining.
