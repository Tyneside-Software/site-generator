# Chapter 21
## Imaginary But Useful

Saturday morning Michael tried to pay a bill and met the square root of minus one in the fine print of civilisation.

Not literally printed—there are limits even to modern invoices—but sitting underneath the systems that moved money, music, and mobile signal from pocket to pocket. He had clicked a help article about a slow payment gateway, fallen through a link about signal processing, and surfaced in a paragraph that treated *i* as if it were as ordinary as a stamp.

Imaginary numbers.

Joshua arrived in person with pastries and the expression of a man who had been waiting for this particular toy to come out of the box.

'√−1,' he said, sitting at the kitchen table. 'Doesn't sit on the everyday line. Engineers use it anyway. Motors spin. Radios sing. The "imaginary" pays real bills.'

Michael bit a croissant and considered the insult buried in the name. 'We call it imaginary like a child inventing dragons. Then we run the grid on dragons.'

'Language lagged. The math was doing work before the branding department approved it. Same as people who say *just a metaphor* about things that rearrange their lives.'

Patterns were stacking without becoming a lecture: wave and point, zero the useful empty, brains demoted to late dessert. Each day of the week handed him a tool that refused the false choice between *real* and *made-up*.

'So usefulness isn't the same as sitting still for a photograph,' Michael said.

'Exactly. Some truths are orthogonal. They don't show up on the first axis you draw, and still hold the bridge up.'

Michael wiped crumbs. 'Cameron would say you're smuggling mysticism through engineering.'

'Cameron's not here yet. When he arrives he can audit the electricity. Until then, the phone in your pocket is a portable shrine to numbers that "don't exist."'

The phone buzzed on cue—an entirely real notification about an unreal-sounding payment clearing. Michael laughed, helpless. The universe had a vaudeville sense of timing.

They walked to the park because kitchens left alone too long with metaphysics start to smell of burnt solemnity. Children ran along paths Euclidean enough for scraped knees. A man flew a drone that obeyed radio waves happily indifferent to philosophical naming conventions.

'I used to think imaginary meant false,' Michael said. 'Then fiction taught me better. Then grief. Then love. Now maths piles on.'

'False is a different department. Imaginary, in this sense, means *rotated*. Turned out of the line you were staring at. Still rigid. Still accountable. Just not flattering your first glance.'

A dog chased a ball into a hedge and emerged with leaves like a green halo. Real dog. Real leaves. The joy was harder to place on a number line and no less factual for the difficulty.

Michael sat on a bench that needed paint. 'Is God imaginary but useful?'

Joshua was quiet long enough for a cyclist to pass. 'Careful. That sentence can mean ten things, eight of them lazy. If you mean "a name we rotate into for work the flat line can't do," maybe. If you mean "a lie that comforts," that's a smaller god than the one who won't unplug when you doubt.'

Michael accepted the correction. Week two was not for crowning answers. It was for collecting honest tools.

On the way back a busker played through a cheap amp—mathematics wearing a human throat. Michael dropped a coin that was not imaginary into a case that was not a metaphor, and felt the transaction complete in every sense that mattered.

At home the payment gateway finally showed a green tick. Somewhere in the deep machinery, numbers that could not be simple had finished a simple job: money moved; a company was paid; a service continued. He stared at the tick longer than was dignified. Green was not a proof of God. Green was a proof that the world ran on tools proud people dismissed as unreal until the lights stayed on.

He opened a music app and played a song he loved for no budget-relevant reason. Compression, sampling, the ghost of mathematics under melody—imaginary cousins paying for joy. Joshua, still at the table with the last pastry, tapped the beat on the wood like a man signing a receipt.

'Thanks for the pastries,' Michael said.

'Thanks for noticing the grid. Most people only notice when it fails. Noticing when it works is gratitude with better accuracy.'

'I used to think accuracy was the enemy of wonder.'

'Only bad accuracy. Good accuracy makes the wonder stop lying.'

Sunday would bring ants and crowds and the question of whether the higher level was less real than the lower. Michael already suspected the answer would refuse to choose. Wave and point had trained him for honest refusal; √−1 was only the latest tutor.

He washed two plates—a thoroughly non-imaginary chore—and let the useful mystery stay useful without demanding it become ordinary in the wrong way. Soap bubbles held brief rainbows and popped without apologising for their geometry. On the windowsill a spider rebuilt a thread the broom had ruined that morning: rotated work, load-bearing, invisible until light caught it.

The ordinary, he was learning, had always been stranger than the slogans allowed—and the strange had always been paying rent in ordinary rooms.
