# Chapter 27
## Keys Six And Seven

Wednesday evening Michael's laptop keyboard became a small geological survey.

He had meant to answer three emails and go to bed at a Cameron-approved hour. Instead he found himself mapping plastic continents, the way lonely children invent countries on bedroom carpets. The difference was that his countries already had symbols printed at the factory.

He was not working. He was procrastinating in the sacred modern way: staring at keys until they confessed. The number row gleamed faintly. Six. Seven. Above them, shifted, the caret and the ampersand—^ and &—neighbours on the plastic continent, forever side by side like a comedy duo sharing a dressing room.

Joshua was on speakerphone from the kitchen sink's white noise.

'You're doing the keyboard stare,' Joshua said. 'Should I be worried?'

'Keys six and seven. Shift six is caret. Shift seven is ampersand. They sit together like they planned it.'

'Everything sits together if you stare long enough. That's how people invent prophecies and also how people invent fonts. What's the load-bearing joke?'

Michael typed ^^^^^^ and &&&&&& and looked at the screen like a man waiting for a burning bush that used monospace.

'Caret points up,' he said. 'Attention. Insert here. The little hat that says *something belongs above*. Ampersand is *and*—the glue, the both, the refusal of lonely nouns. Side by side on every cheap keyboard in the country.'

'Funny dual. Not The Answer. Funny dual. Keep the crown in the drawer.'

Michael exhaled. After Cameron's sandpaper and the plank verse, he could hear the overshoot instinct revving. 'I'm not crowning. I'm noticing adjacency. Wave and point had a pub. These have a key row.'

'Good. Adjacency is allowed. Coronation requires a committee we don't have.'

Michael pasted ^& into a blank document and laughed despite himself. It looked like a secret handshake drawn by a programmer who had been awake too long. It looked like a cartoon shrug. It looked like both and a hat.

'People will think I'm unwell.'

'People think anyone who loves punctuation is unwell. Show Cameron. If he doesn't hang up, you're still in the safe zone.'

Cameron, when shown a photo of the keys later that evening over coffee, did not hang up. He did something better: he raised one eyebrow the precise distance of a man declining a cult application.

'If you make that your religion I will go to bed early out of spite,' Cameron said. 'Also mildly funny. Like a logo for a band that never tours.'

'Not religion. Neighbours on plastic.'

'Keep it as neighbours. The second you call it the axis of reality I bring the plank verse back.'

Joshua, from the next chair, raised his cup. 'Amen to neighbours.'

Michael made tea and used the kettle's time to remember other duals that had behaved this week without demanding thrones: marmalade and jam, wave and point, zero and the hotel's infinite rooms, ants and colony. The keyboard was only the latest pair of socks in the cosmic laundry—matched, mundane, oddly perfect.

A work email arrived with a subject line full of ampersands: *Q3 & planning & owners*. The world ran on *&* without spiritual crisis. Children learned ^ as a hat in maths before they learned it as a spear of meaning. Michael pictured a classroom of eight-year-olds drawing little roofs over numbers, none of them aware they were handling a future mystic's favourite toy. The thought kept him humble. Toys became idols only when adults forgot they had been toys.

He typed a reply that contained neither symbol and felt virtuous, then undid the virtue by muttering to the empty room: 'They're still funny.'

He walked to the shop for milk he had bought yesterday and did not need, purely to put distance between his eyes and the key row. On the way back a bus display scrolled *&* between destination names like a tiny secular liturgy. He laughed, paid for nothing extra, and returned to the laptop like a man returning to a friend who might become a problem.

Before bed he cleaned the keyboard with a tissue—a priestly act. Dust came away from between six and seven like minor sins. The keys remained what they were: input devices. Also, if you were the sort of person Michael was becoming, a joke the universe told with QWERTY—softly, without demanding genuflection.

He did not yet know how hard he would push ^& tomorrow in an essay that overshot.

Tonight he only knew they sat together—

—and that sitting together, on a keyboard or a road of different speeds, was already a kind of quiet gospel.
