# Chapter 69
## Scripture Reasoning

The room at the interfaith centre smelled of carpet cleaner and hope, which is a difficult blend and somehow worked.

Chairs in a circle. A low table. Printed sheets with short passages—Hebrew, Greek-in-translation, Arabic-in-translation—laid out like place settings for a meal nobody would win. No one sat at a head. The facilitator, a woman with the calm of someone who had chaired worse storms, explained the rules as if they were oxygen:

Read. Listen. Do not fix. Do not recruit. Speak from your own hearing. Leave space.

Michael sat between Joshua and an empty chair that later filled with a rabbi who arrived late and apologising, bicycle helmet under one arm. R. sat opposite, not next to him—on purpose, he suspected, and approved. Distance can be hospitality. Cameron had sent a text: *Here in spirit at forty-two decibels. Don’t crown a verse.*

They began with a short text about mercy.

Michael’s hands wanted to compare. His mouth wanted to synthesise. The recognition of recent days wanted a speaking role, like an actor who has just had a good review and cannot stop mentioning it. He held still and did the harder thing: he listened to a stranger say what the words did in *her* chest, not in his system.

An older Christian man spoke of debt cancelled—voice rough, as if the word still cost him something. A young Muslim woman spoke of compassion written near the beginning of every chapter of her life of prayer; she did not perform certainty; she performed attention. The rabbi spoke of walking after the divine with the awkward gait of people who argue as love. R. said little at first, then: ‘When we read together like this, I stop auditioning for who wins the library.’

Joshua murmured amen without making it a takeover. Amen, properly used, is a door, not a flag.

Michael’s turn came. He felt the temptation to unload the week—the field, the joke, the napkin, the whole private weather system. He looked at the page instead. The paper was cheap. The invitation was not.

‘I hear,’ he said carefully, ‘an instruction not to treat mercy as a scarce resource. My habit is to map. Mapping can become a way of not being changed. Tonight I’m trying to be changed before I’m clever.’

The facilitator nodded as if he had handed in homework on time. No gold star. Just room to keep going.

They moved to a second passage. Disagreement appeared—real, not decorative. Someone flinched at a translation and named the flinch. Someone else owned a colonial hangover in how their tradition had taught them to hear others; the ownership was quiet and costlier than a slogan. The air tightened, then eased, because the rules held. Rules, Michael thought, were not the enemy of spirit; they were the bannister on a dark stair.

At the tea break he found R. by the urn. Plastic cups. Biscuits of indeterminate faith. Joshua arrived with three of them and the distribution ethics of a man who had been trained by children and saints, categories that sometimes overlapped.

‘This is slower than my brain likes,’ Michael said.

‘That’s the point,’ R. said, stirring sugar she did not always take. ‘Your brain just wrote a novel in a fortnight. Scripture Reasoning is the anti-novel: short pages, long ears. You don’t get to be the protagonist every sentence.’

Joshua offered biscuits. ‘I like that no one gets to be the final editor in the room.’

‘Don’t get comfortable,’ R. said. ‘Final editors exist. Just not here, for an hour, if we behave. Behaving is a spiritual practice people underrate because it doesn’t photograph well.’

Cameron’s text arrived: *Still not converted. Proud of you if you shut up occasionally.*

Michael showed them. They laughed into their cups—the soft laugh of people who needed the sandpaper and knew it.

In the second half a passage about speech and intention nearly derailed into politics. The facilitator steered with a question: *What does this ask of you this week, not of the government?* People exhaled. Specifics returned: a hard conversation with a sibling; a refusal to share a cruel joke; a decision to visit someone boring on purpose, which is a kind of courage the internet never trends.

Michael thought of beams and fields and monograms and did not say the phrases. He said: ‘I need to stop using other people’s books as mirrors for my recent cleverness.’

R. met his eyes across the circle and gave the smallest nod—permission without applause.

When the hour ended, chairs scraped. Papers were stacked. No one had been won. Several people had been slightly rearranged, which is the only conversion worth having in a carpeted room on a weeknight. The rabbi collected his helmet. The older Christian man shook the young Muslim woman’s hand with the awkward grace of someone learning a new dance late in life.

Outside, under a streetlight that did not care about religions, Joshua squeezed Michael’s shoulder.

‘You didn’t perform,’ he said. ‘Well done. Performing is easy. Not performing is a craft.’

R. zipped her coat. ‘Same time next week if you can keep not performing. I’ll know if you slip.’

‘I can try,’ Michael said.

They parted three ways into the same city. Behind them the centre lights went off section by section, like a book closing without claiming it was the only book.

Michael walked home with the passages still in his pocket. On the bus he did not re-read them as trophies. He let them sit as guests who had shared a circle and refused to become his private alphabet—still speaking their own languages, still owed the dignity of not being translated into *his* point before the night had cooled.
