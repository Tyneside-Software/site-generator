# Chapter 7
## Both/And Shop Opens

The pub on Tuesday night was doing what pubs have always done: converting the end of a day into something with cushions.

Michael found Joshua already at a corner table with two pints, a stack of beer mats, and a black pen that had seen wars. Joshua’s coat was draped over the chair like a sleeping dog. The fruit machine in the corner flashed the colours of a small, desperate carnival.

‘Right,’ Joshua said. ‘Shop name.’

He wrote on a mat, block letters, the pen squeaking:

**BOTH/AND**  
**Open late.**  
**Closed to false dilemmas.**

Michael turned the mat around. ‘You’re going to get us banned for unlicensed metaphysics.’

‘We’ll pay in peanuts and sincerity. Stock list next.’

They made one. It was not a good business plan. It was better than that.

Joshua wrote, and Michael amended, and the mats became a kind of inventory spoken aloud as much as inked: wave *and* point; free *and* caused; sacred *and* ordinary; tired *and* not done; sure *and* still listening; one room *and* many doors; everything fits *and* not everything is fine.

‘That last one,’ Michael said. ‘That’s the non-mush clause.’

‘That’s the fire-is-not-a-houseplant clause. Both/and is not a smoothie. It’s refusing to let a false fork own your feet while still knowing pans can be hot.’

A man at the next table glanced over, decided they were planning a band name, and returned to his crossword.

Michael drank. The pint was honest. ‘This morning on the high street I wanted to hand both of them a mat. Preacher gets *sacred and ordinary*. Sceptic gets *reason and wonder*, or whatever the non-weaponised version is. Instead I ate a sandwich and felt superior for eleven seconds, which is also a product we should not stock.’

Joshua wrote on a fresh mat: **Superiority — out of stock permanently.**

‘Good policy.’

They played the headline game, an old brother game: say the thing in a way that would fit on a shop sign without lying. Joshua won when he looked at Michael—really looked, the way family does when the week has been heavy—and wrote:

**Tired and not done**  
**— about you, that**

Michael felt the hit land cleanly, without cruelty. ‘Rude.’

‘Accurate. You’ve been awake since Monday’s axiom like a man holding a live wire and calling it a hobby. The shop sells permission to be unfinished without being fake.’

Michael turned a blank mat in his fingers. The cardboard was soft at the edges from a previous drinker’s life. He wrote, slowly:

**Is *and* is becoming**

Joshua raised an eyebrow. ‘That’s either deep or the pint talking.’

‘Both. Annoyingly.’

They ordered chips to share, because philosophy without chips is just anxiety with better vocabulary. When the food came—hot, slightly too salty, perfect—they ate with the focus of people who have remembered they have bodies. Salt on fingers; vinegar in the air; the small peace of a shared plate. Around them the pub did its layered work: laughter, a quiet argument about football that would never be resolved and did not need to be, someone singing one line of a song and then having the grace to stop.

‘Does the shop have a till?’ Michael asked.

‘Pay what you can. Mostly you pay by not amputating the world for comfort.’

‘Opening hours?’

‘Whenever someone is about to say *either you believe X or you’re an idiot*. We open in their face like a pop-up and offer a third shelf.’

Michael laughed properly then, the kind that unhooks something in the shoulders. ‘We’re going to be so popular. People love being offered a third shelf.’

‘They hate it,’ Joshua said cheerfully. ‘Then, six months later, they realise their feet still work and send a thank-you card written in grumpy handwriting.’

They left the mats in a small stack by the ketchup, a ridiculous shrine. Joshua kept the shop-sign mat and tucked it into his coat pocket.

‘For the fridge. Between the takeaway menu and the electricity bill. Sacred and ordinary.’

Outside, the night was cold enough to make breath visible. They walked as far as the corner together. Michael could still hear, faintly, the fruit machine celebrating someone else’s near-miss.

‘Thank you,’ he said. ‘For the shop. For the sandwich coffee earlier. For not letting me join an army for the vibe.’

Joshua shrugged, which is how he accepted thanks. ‘Tomorrow you’ll be back in the laptop mines. Try not to either/or your own code into a corner. And text me if the universe opens a franchise.’

‘Both/And doesn’t franchise. It just keeps being true in other people’s kitchens.’

They parted. Michael walked home with his hands in his pockets and the odd sense that a shop had opened which had always been there—only now it had a name, a stock list, and a closing policy that forbade false dilemmas from camping on the doorstep.

On his own fridge later he stuck nothing—because the mat was at Joshua’s—but he stood in the kitchen light and recited the sign under his breath anyway, like a man locking up for the night.

Open late. Closed to false dilemmas. Stock: the world, uncut.
