# Chapter 36
## Omega Isn’t A Job

Once you pull on a thread marked *alpha*, the word *omega* comes away in your hand like a second sock.

Michael spent the afternoon with the less glamorous half of the myth.

In the popular story, omega was a job description. Lowest rung. Scapegoat. Stress-sponge. Sometimes jester, sometimes punchbag, always fixed—as if a living creature had been hired into a permanent role in someone else’s hierarchy and given no exit interview. Leadership blogs loved it. People who had been hurt loved having a word for the bottom of the pile, which was understandable and still not the same as zoology.

The better wolf science was dryer and kinder. A fixed omega role looked a lot like something that appears when you jam unrelated animals into a space too small for their nerves. Captivity manufactures politics. Stress invents jobs. The scapegoat is often an artefact of a bad system, not a natural vocation written into fur.

Michael paced the living room with a pen behind his ear. The empty chair by the window sometimes stood in for Joshua and sometimes for God. Today it was just a chair.

“So omega isn’t a career,” he told it. “It’s what a broken arrangement does to whoever can’t leave.”

He texted Joshua anyway.

Michael: *Omega as fixed scapegoat = captivity artefact. Not a sacred job title.*

Joshua: *Then stop volunteering for it.*

Michael stared at the reply. Brothers have a way of translating cosmology into homework.

He typed, deleted, typed:

Michael: *I don’t volunteer—*

Joshua: *You take the hit so the room stays calm. You carry the intensity alone. You call it the hard path. Sometimes that’s vocation. Sometimes that’s you accepting a role a stressed pack invented because no one redesigned the cage.*

The message sat there, glowing with the particular light of sentences that are true enough to sting.

Cameron, inevitably:

Cameron: *If omega means ‘person who absorbs everyone else’s chaos’, that’s not mysticism, that’s a boundary problem.*

Michael: *I’m writing about wolves.*

Cameron: *You’re always writing about wolves. Set a calendar alert for ‘not my job’.*

Michael laughed despite himself and put the phone face down. The laugh left a clean space in the room, like a window opened.

He made tea properly this time—mug, bag, patience—and stood at the counter reconstructing the both/and without forcing a sermon.

The cultural omega was real as story: people got cast as lowest, treated as lowest, believed they were lowest. Stories shape behaviour. That mattered.

The scientific omega, for wild families, was mostly not a permanent identity. Young disperse. Roles flex. The pack is kinship more than corporate chart.

And somewhere beyond both, the old sacred sense of last things—endings, completions, the final letter in someone else’s alphabet—waited without needing him to pin it to a victim.

He did not need to decode every Greek ghost today. He only needed one clean thought: omega was not a job he owed the universe. Scapegoat was what systems did when they refused to grow up.

Joshua rang. The phone buzzed against the wood like a small impatient animal.

“You angry at me?” Joshua asked.

“No,” Michael said. “Annoyed at accuracy.”

“Good. Listen—there’s a difference between serving and being the designated shock absorber. One is love. The other is a captivity script with better PR. You can walk the hard path without applying for omega on LinkedIn.”

Michael looked out at the street. A kid on a scooter practised falling and getting up, which is the only leadership model that ever reliably works. A neighbour dragged a recycling bin with the ceremonial seriousness of late empire.

“I keep thinking ranks are both real and fake,” he said. “People act ranks. Families aren’t ladders. Stress makes ladders. The words float between all of it.”

“Both/and,” Joshua said. “Shop’s still open. Just don’t stock self-erasure under ‘loyalty’.”

After the call he stuck a single sticky note near the desk: *Omega is not a permanent vacancy for your suffering.*

He felt lighter, which was suspicious and welcome.

In the evening a message arrived that was really a transfer of someone else’s panic—urgency without ownership, a room looking for a free body to stand under a leak. He declined it politely. No speech. No wolf metaphors. The other person sounded briefly surprised, then adapted, which is what adults do when the free shock-absorber goes offline.

The world did not end.

Which suggested, as data points go, that omega had never been a job description issued by reality—only a story some rooms told when they wanted a body under the drip instead of mending the roof.

Michael washed the mug. Small acts of not being a sponge. Later he sat on the sofa with the television off and listened to the house make ordinary noises—pipes, a car door, someone laughing two gardens over.

He slept without auditioning for the bottom rung. The pack, such as it was, would have to share the weather.
