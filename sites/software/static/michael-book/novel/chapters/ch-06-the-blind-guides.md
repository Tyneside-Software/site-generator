# Chapter 6
## The Blind Guides

Tuesday lunchtime the high street performed its usual miracle: too many people in too little pavement, all of them convinced they were the main character.

Michael had ducked out for a sandwich and air. He got both, plus a free demonstration of the human talent for forking the world into two wrong roads and then fighting to the death over which fork was holy.

Near the cash machine a man with a portable amplifier was explaining that you were either saved or lost, awake or asleep, with God or against Him, and that the sandwich shop queue did not count as a third option. His voice had the scraped brightness of someone who had replaced conversation with volume.

Ten yards away, as if placed by a director with a sense of humour, a woman in a charcoal coat was telling her friend—loudly enough for the pavement to audit—that all of it was nonsense, that religion was a virus, that anyone still talking about God in the twenty-first century needed a hobby and possibly a therapist. Her certainty matched the preacher’s decibel for decibel. Only the logo on the banner differed.

Michael stood between them with a paper bag and a packet of crisps, a neutral nation with snacks.

The preacher pointed at the sky. The sceptic pointed at her phone, where a quote about reason glowed like a relic. A bus hissed. A child asked for an ice cream and was told later, which is how empires fall and also how afternoons continue.

He bought the sandwich—cheese and pickle, the food of people who have decided not to be interesting about lunch—and found a low wall that was not currently hosting lost gloves. He ate and listened. Not because either speech was good. Because the *shape* of them was identical.

Either/or. Pick a team. The other team is not merely mistaken; they are defective.

Joshua appeared as if conjured by the smell of pickle. He had a coffee in each hand and the look of a man who had seen the amplifier from a distance and come to prevent a scene.

‘I wasn’t going to argue,’ Michael said.

‘You were going to *think* an argument so hard the air would bruise. Same crime, quieter weapon.’

They sat. The preacher hit a patch about lakes of fire. The sceptic hit a patch about flying spaghetti. A pigeon investigated a chip with the deep focus of a scholar.

‘They’re both doing the same trick,’ Michael said. ‘Selling a fork. Left path or right path. Heaven-or-nothing. Reason-or-nothing. As if the street only had two directions and both of them came with a membership card.’

‘Blind guides,’ Joshua said. ‘Not because they’re stupid. Because they’re pointing so hard at their preferred half that they can’t see the road is wider than the pointing.’

Michael took a bite. Pickle did its sharp little job. ‘Yesterday I tried to prove pure nothing can’t sit opposite existence. Today I’m watching people invent opposite teams so they don’t have to live in a world that holds both hymns and hypotheses without exploding.’

‘Both/and frightens people. It sounds like mush. Like you won’t take a side when a side is needed. Refusing a *false* fork isn’t mush. It’s declining to amputate your feet to fit a shoe advert.’

The sceptic’s friend said something mild about not knowing. She was ignored by both poles with the efficiency of a weather system ignoring a single umbrella.

Michael felt a flash of kinship with the friend. Not-knowing was not a team. It was a weather report.

‘The annoying museum from this morning,’ he said. ‘Everything fits. Including preachers. Including sceptics. Including my irritation. The either/or is a story told *inside* the museum, not a wall of the building.’

Joshua nodded at the amplifier. ‘He’s not wrong that people sleepwalk. She’s not wrong that people weaponise certainty. The blindness is the part where each one needs the other to be empty of truth so their own half can feel complete.’

‘So what’s the alternative? Standing in the middle looking smug?’

‘Standing in the middle looking *hungry*. Hungry for what’s true on both sides and allergic to the bit where the sides demand you hate the furniture.’

The preacher finished with an invitation. A few people took leaflets. Most people took their shopping and their lives. The sceptic and her friend moved on toward a shop selling shoes that promised transformation. The pavement exhaled.

Michael folded the sandwich paper into a careful square, the way he folded ideas he intended to keep.

‘I keep wanting a villain. A clean enemy of the good. Instead I get two sales pitches and a pigeon.’

‘Villains are easy. Sales pitches are honest about being sales pitches if you listen with your whole head. Come on. You’ve got work. I’ve got a beer mat to vandalise later.’

‘The bad idea?’

‘The bad idea. We’re going to open a shop that doesn’t sell either/or. On cardboard. In a pub. Very low rent.’

Michael stood, coffee warm through the cup, the street ordinary again. The amplifier was being packed away into a rucksack that had known better gigs. Somewhere a till drawer rang. A cyclist swore gently at a delivery van and both of them continued to exist without forming a denomination about it.

He walked back toward the office with the taste of pickle still bright on his tongue. The high street did not thank him for declining its fork. It rarely thanks anyone. That was fine. He still had both hands free for the next true thing, and the road—annoyingly, mercifully—was wider than either of the banners had been willing to admit.
