# Chapter 43
## Four Twenty And Other Appointed Hours

At 16:20 the document saved itself with a little digital sigh, and Michael’s phone—which had opinions about everything—displayed the time in a font that made the joke unavoidable.

4:20.

He stared. Then he laughed, then he felt foolish for laughing, then he laughed at the foolishness, which is a kind of recursion the universe seems to enjoy.

He was not high. He was not in a dorm. He was a middle-aged man with a half-written chapter about moral landfills and a calendar full of meetings that did not care about cosmology. And still the clock had arrived like a comedian who knew the room.

Joshua answered on the second ring. “If this is an emergency, say emergency. If this is a sign, say sign and prepare to be teased.”

“Four twenty,” Michael said. “I hit save. The phone looked at me like a conspirator.”

“Appointed hour,” Joshua said, delighted. “Or confirmation bias with good comic timing. Both can buy a round.”

“I’m trying not to build a religion out of a digital clock.”

“Good. Religions built out of digital clocks tend to have terrible hymns.” Joshua’s voice softened. “But you can let it be funny without making it a master key. Humour is allowed to touch the sacred without annexing it.”

Michael walked to the window. School-run weather. On a bus glass across the street a child’s sun-with-a-face was already sliding into abstract art—yellow smeared by heat and someone else’s sleeve. The world kept appointing hours that meant nothing and everything depending on how hungry you were for meaning.

He made tea because 4:20 is a fine time for tea if you refuse the other sacrament. The kettle’s click was more honest than most omens: heat, water, done.

On a scrap of paper he listed, not as proof, as weather report, the *appointed hours* the week had already offered without asking permission:

The night no outside left, while washing a plate.  
The morning the sin category cracked.  
The pastry hour of verse forty-two.  
Now a stoner punchline wearing a wristwatch.

None of them proved a committee beyond the sky. All of them were *available*—moments when attention and event shook hands. You could call that God. You could call it pattern-hungry primates. You could call it both and go make dinner.

Michael tried the third option, which was becoming a habit.

Cameron appeared in the doorway of the evening with a bottle of something soft and a face ready to mock. “Your ears burned. Mine burned. What have you done.”

“Clock said 4:20 while I saved a file about not inventing landfills for sin.”

Cameron sat without being invited, which was friendship. “Of course it did. The cosmos does stand-up for people who stare at it too long. Hydrate.”

“That’s your pastoral care?”

“That’s my entire theology.” Cameron cracked the bottle. “Working rule, please, before you start a newsletter.”

Michael said it out loud so it would cost something: “Appointed hours are invitations, not warrants. I don’t get to arrest people with my coincidences.”

Cameron pointed at him with the bottle neck. “Frame that. If you use a funny clock to win an argument with someone who is merely tired, I will personally introduce you to a wall.”

They drank the soft stuff. Outside, streetlights came on in their appointed sequence, which is to say: the council’s timer, not destiny, and still beautiful if you were in the mood.

“I nearly made it a proof,” Michael admitted. “In my head. For two seconds. *See? Even the phone agrees.* That is how cults start—two seconds of agreement with a device.”

“Cults start earlier,” Cameron said. “With loneliness that wants a jury. The clock is just available casting.”

Michael cooked pasta while Cameron supervised with the moral authority of a man who did not live here and therefore knew better. Salt. Steam. A sauce that had been a jar fifteen minutes ago and was now, technically, dinner. They ate at the counter. No candles. No ceremony. The scrap of appointed hours sat under a mug like evidence that had already been downgraded to anecdote.

“Does it still feel holy?” Cameron asked, mid-fork.

“It feels *funny*,” Michael said. “Holy is a promotion I don’t get to hand out to every coincidence that winks. Funny is allowed. Funny doesn’t arrest anyone.”

“You’re almost house-trained,” Cameron said, and stole a piece of penne.

Joshua rang while the washing-up water ran. “Did the hour survive contact with Cameron?”

“Survived,” Michael said. “Demoted from warrant to wink. Tea at 4:20. Hymns cancelled. Pasta as penance.”

“Amen to cancelled hymns,” Joshua said. “And Michael—if tomorrow at 4:20 nothing happens, that is also data. Silence is not a failed appointment. It’s the field not performing for you.”

“That might be the hardest rule,” Michael said.

“Good,” Joshua said. “Hard rules are the ones that keep you kind.”

Before bed Michael set an ordinary alarm for an ordinary morning. No sacred minute. No conspiratorial save. Just sleep, which is also an appointed hour if you are generous, and merely biology if you are not—and either way, the phone would still light up with a number that someone, somewhere, would find funny.

He left the scrap under the mug so the window could open without the week’s weather blowing away.

Invitations, not warrants.

The night, being large, allowed both the wink and the silence after it—and did not require him to choose which one was God.
