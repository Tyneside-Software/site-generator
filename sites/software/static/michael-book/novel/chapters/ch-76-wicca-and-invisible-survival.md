# Chapter 76
## Wicca And Invisible Survival

The conversation began because a teenager on the bus was reading a book with a pentacle on the cover and looking ready to fight the whole vehicle if anyone smirked.

Michael did not smirk. He remembered being a teenager with worse symbols and less courage. The bus hissed at a stop; a woman with shopping bags glanced at the cover, chose neutrality, and sat. The teenager’s shoulders dropped half an inch. Survival, Michael thought, sometimes looked like a spine that expected mockery and received none.

He got off two stops early on purpose and walked, turning the thought over like a coin with no official currency. Rain had left the pavement honest. A charity shop window held a plastic angel, a chipped mug, and a dreamcatcher that had seen better dreams. He did not invent a theology for the window. He only noticed that not everything for sale had a cathedral’s PR budget.

Later, in Joshua’s kitchen, while potatoes boiled like small negotiators, he said, ‘I’ve been thinking about people who kept practices under the radar. Not as a romance of the occult. As survival.’

Joshua salted water. ‘Go on. The pan is listening.’

‘Empire religions get buildings,’ Michael said. ‘They get holidays off work. They get to be the default on forms. Other paths—old village habits, cunning folk, modern Wicca, things half-invented and half-remembered—learn to be quiet. Quiet isn’t always shame. Sometimes it’s how you keep the thread when the loud gods have police.’

Joshua stirred. ‘You’re not about to declare yourself high priest of the saucepan.’

‘I’m about to not erase the under-radar just because my week gave me a loud recognition,’ Michael said. ‘Invisible survival is part of the field too. If there’s no outside, the hidden is still here. It just learned not to advertise.’

He fetched plates without being asked. The kitchen window showed the neighbour’s washing line: shirts, a tea towel with a faded flag, something glittery that might have been a child’s cape or a ritual robe. He refused to decide. Deciding without asking was how museums invented savages.

Joshua tipped the potatoes into a colander. Steam rose like a small weather system with no empire. ‘One idea only,’ he said. ‘Honour the skill. Don’t recruit the aesthetic. Discernment later with Cameron’s sandpaper.’

‘Agreed.’

R., when he floated a careful version of this on a walk days later, did not flinch into rivalry. She walked as if the pavement owed her nothing and the argument owed her precision.

‘Islam has been empire and minority depending on the map,’ she said. ‘I know what it is to be loud in one country and a problem in another. I won’t romanticise every hidden path as pure. Some hidden things are hidden because they harm. But yes—survival under pressure is a human skill. Don’t only study cathedrals.’

They paused at a crossing while a hearse and a delivery van negotiated the same junction with different kinds of urgency. Michael said, ‘I used to file anything with candles under *not serious*. That was snobbery dressed as rationality.’

‘Snobbery is a loud god too,’ R. said. ‘It also has police—just social ones. They don’t arrest. They smirk. Same spine-drop as your bus, different uniform.’

‘I don’t want to make the under-radar my new mascot either,’ Michael said. ‘Mascots are just loud gods with better merch.’

‘Then study without annexing,’ she said. ‘Look. Don’t collect. Don’t baptise a market stall into your monogram’s supporting cast.’

Cameron’s contribution arrived by text after Michael overshared in the group chat: *Also, some people are “under the radar” because they’re making it up as they go and hoping aesthetic saves them. Discernment, please. Not every candle is a lineage.*

Michael read that aloud to Joshua over the potatoes. Joshua laughed into the steam. ‘Blessed are the discerning, for they shall not buy every pendant.’

They ate with butter and the humility of food that began as dirt. Michael told a story of a woman he’d met years ago at a market stall—crystals, yes, but also a practical kindness, a refusal to curse anyone for cash, a clear-eyed *I do this because it helps me not die inside, not because I own the moon*. He had filed her under ‘not my tribe’ then. He filed her under ‘person’ now.

‘What did you buy?’ Joshua asked.

‘Nothing. I was rude with my smile. I want to go back and buy the cheap thing and say thank you for existing without a grant.’

‘Syncretism later,’ Joshua warned. ‘Tonight is only the honour due to survival.’

‘Agreed. One idea.’ Michael raised his water glass. ‘To those who kept a practice alive without a grant application.’

‘And to those who left harmful practices behind without a parade,’ Joshua added. ‘Both survivals. The second one doesn’t get enough toasts.’

That night Michael walked home past a church, a closed shopfront that had once been something else, a flat with a small rainbow sticker, a window with a lamp and a silhouette watering a plant. The city was full of private altars: football, children, work, grief, God, gods, none of the above. A fox crossed the road with the confidence of the unlisted.

He did not add any of it to the napkin in his wallet.

He only let the thought stand: a man who has just seen a bright symbol must practise not needing every other light to dim so his can look like the sun.

Harriet was still up when he came in, halfway through a documentary about bees. She muted it.

‘You look like you’ve been arguing with a cathedral,’ she said.

‘With myself,’ Michael said. ‘I won on points. The cathedral remains undefeated on architecture.’

She patted the sofa. He sat. On screen, bees worked a hive that needed no grant, no holiday, no form where *other* was a polite way of saying *we didn’t plan for you*.

‘Invisible doesn’t mean unreal,’ he said.

‘Neither does loud,’ she said. ‘Pass me the remote. Your sermon is interrupting the bees.’

He passed it. She unmuted. For a while they watched small bodies do large work in silence that was not shame, only method.

Outside, the fox had already moved on. Unlisted, and still alive.
