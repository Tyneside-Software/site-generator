# Chapter 35
## Alpha Is Mum And Dad

Saturday morning, Michael fell down a research hole that began with a throwaway phrase and ended with wolves.

Someone online had used *alpha* the way people always used it—chin out, hierarchy porn, the fantasy of a ladder you climb by biting. Michael, who had been thinking about first and last things more than was strictly professional, typed a question he should have typed years ago:

Do wild wolves actually work like that?

The answer, when the better sources arrived, was the sort of correction that makes popular culture look like it had been drinking.

The rigid alpha–omega ladder had been built largely out of mid-century studies of captive wolves—unrelated animals forced together, stressed, inventing politics the way prisoners invent currencies. A famous book had helped the myth travel. Leadership seminars had rented the myth a suit. Dog trainers had turned it into a religion of dominance. Meanwhile, in the actual woods, packs were mostly families.

Mum. Dad. Kids.

The breeding pair were not tyrants who had fought their way up a corporate structure made of teeth. They were parents. Youngsters dispersed when they grew. The word *alpha*, applied like a badge of conquest, was a mistranslation of kinship into empire.

Michael sat back from the screen and laughed once, short and delighted.

Of course. Of course the universe’s favourite joke would be that the word people used for domination was, in better light, a word for looking after the den.

Joshua came in with two mugs and the expression of a man who had heard laughter and wanted to know if it was safe. Steam rose between them. The kitchen smelled of coffee and the faint ghost of toast.

“Wolves,” Michael said. “Alpha isn’t the boss fight. Alpha is mum and dad.”

Joshua handed him a mug. “Say more.”

Michael turned the laptop. A careful paragraph about L. David Mech, about later corrections, about retiring the language for wild animals. Joshua read, eyebrows climbing, then whistled soft through his teeth.

“So the internet’s favourite masculinity cosplay,” Joshua said, “is a captivity artefact.”

“Mostly. Some researchers still say alpha pair casually. Still not the ladder myth.” Michael sipped. “I love it. The symbol people use for first and last, top and bottom, turns out to be family science wearing a Halloween costume called hierarchy.”

Joshua smiled. “Both/and shop opens again.”

“The myth is culturally real,” Michael said, ticking fingers against the mug. “People behave as if the ladder is true. The science says the ladder is largely wrong for the animals they stole it from. And then the same letters show up in other rooms—beginnings and endings, titles, claims about the whole.” He stopped himself before the sentence ran into later chapters. Timing was a kind of ethics.

Cameron texted into the void that friends maintain between each other:

Cameron: *If this becomes a TED talk about how you’re the dad-wolf of Tyneside I’m leaving the chat.*

Michael: *It’s about bad science becoming folklore. And family being the real pack.*

Cameron: *Fine. Still not calling you alpha.*

Joshua read over his shoulder. “Healthy boundary.”

They took the walk they took when ideas needed legs—path by the field, wet grass, a sky that could not decide between rain and performance. On the path a man barked orders at a bewildered spaniel in the name of dominance theory. The spaniel, who had never read a paper, elected to sniff a bin with the serene authority of someone who knew what actually mattered.

“There,” Michael said quietly. “Captive myth, live demo.”

Joshua watched the spaniel win without fighting. “Mum and dad would have just fed it and set a boundary. Less theatre. More pack.”

Michael thought of his own household—the negotiations, the tired evenings, the fierce protectiveness that did not need a crown. He thought of the hard path and the easy email and the way leadership language always tried to recruit animal blood for human ego.

Alpha, cleaned of the cosplay, was not a permission slip to dominate.

It was a reminder that the ones at the front of a healthy group are often the ones carrying food, teaching, staying when staying costs.

He said as much. Joshua bumped his shoulder, brother shorthand for *landed*.

A child on a scooter practised falling and getting up. Hierarchy would have ranked the speed. Kinship ranked the getting up.

On the walk home the word sat differently in his mouth. Less armour. More apron. The culture would keep selling the ladder; ladders sell. But he had seen the family underneath, and once you see the den, the throne looks like furniture someone dragged into the wrong forest.

At the gate Joshua said, “You’re going to connect this to the big letters eventually.”

“Eventually,” Michael said. “Not before the noticing is clean. Today is only the den.”

“Good. First things first is also a kind of alpha.”

Michael groaned. Joshua looked pleased with himself, which is a brother’s right under common law.

Inside, the next useful act was not symbolism. It was making lunch—sandwiches, cheese that needed using, the unglamorous sacrament of packs that intend to stay together past the seminar.

Joshua took mustard without asking. Michael did not object. That, too, was pack: small invasions of the fridge as proof of belonging.

They ate standing up, plates on the counter, no speech about wolves. Outside, the spaniel’s owner was still calling commands into a universe that preferred bins. Inside, the den held.
