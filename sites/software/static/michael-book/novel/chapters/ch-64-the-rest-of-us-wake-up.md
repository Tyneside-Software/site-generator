# Chapter 64
## The Rest Of Us Wake Up

Morning did its usual miracle of pretending to be new.

Michael woke and, for once, did not rush the experiment of trying not to be here. He let here be here. Cold floor. Soft electric hum. The monogram on the wall. The notebook fat with a week that had refused burial. Tea first, for one body, then the phone—not as a pulpit, as a kitchen table that happened to fit in a pocket.

*I think the next bit isn’t about me noticing,* he sent. *It’s about the rest of us. Reader included. Wake gently.*

Joshua: *Yes. Invite, don’t conscript. Pub table later. Ordinary light.*

Cameron: *If you’re about to found a morning cult, I want biscuits and a right of refusal.*

Michael: *Biscuits granted. Refusal sacred.*

Cameron: *Then proceed.*

He walked the long route through town with no headphones. Faces. Dogs. A delivery driver who did not know he had once been a laboratory for Michael’s dying exile-fantasy. Shop shutters going up like eyelids. The rest of us, waking in the ordinary sense—and maybe, under that, in the sense the book needed.

Not: *you idiots finally see.*  
Yes: *you were always in the room; the room is what is; the jokes were load-bearing; the poles are both; the name of the whole can look like how life continues; there is no final elsewhere to dump the unbearable—including you when you fail.*

He stopped outside the community hall. Light off. Still a doorway. He did not need it open to know the category held. A pigeon inspected the threshold with professional interest and left without converting.

Joshua met him at the scarred pub table that had survived more revelations than polish. Same corner. Same brewery logo on the napkins—cousins of the one still folded in Michael’s pocket like a treaty. Michael almost laughed at the continuity; then he did laugh, and Joshua looked pleased.

“For the ones still asleep,” Joshua said. “Not insult—description. I slept through decades of my own life in good company.”

Michael turned a mug. Steam rose like a small weather system. “I don’t get to crow. Verse forty-two is still in my eye. I only get to set the table.”

“Then set it.”

Michael spoke plain, the hardest dialect, practising for a final chapter that would need the same muscles tomorrow:

“Your experience of this moment exists. That’s the only thing that doesn’t need a committee. From there the chain we’ve walked—nothing as true outside, no pure cut, hell-as-exit failing, sin-as-standing-outside failing, generative poles both, total without a locker room, monogram with two altars in one ink—isn’t a private fever. It’s available. Recognition, not recruitment. You can refuse. Refusal happens *here* too.”

Joshua’s eyes shone with that brother-look: pride mixed with *finally*.

“And the dirty bit,” Joshua said, fondly, “because God is not a coward.”

Michael almost bottled it. Bookstore shelves are full of books that almost bottled it.

He didn’t—not entirely. He tapped the napkin in his pocket through the cloth. “Tomorrow we say it with the drawing on the table and Cameron’s empty chair for the court that keeps us honest. Tonight I only wake the corridor. The rest of us—including me every morning—get to notice what cannot be un-noticed once seen.”

A woman at the next table glanced over, decided this was either a book club or a soft coup, and returned to her scone. Michael silently dedicated the chapter to her—the patron saint of people who stay for the jam and leave the metaphysics on the side plate.

His phone stirred.

Cameron: *I felt a disturbance in the sarcasm. You nearly preached.*

Michael: *Nearly. Invitation only. Biscuits tomorrow if you come.*

Cameron: *I’ll come. Empty chair optional. Someone has to say motte-and-bailey at the end so the novel doesn’t get drunk on itself.*

Michael: *You’re part of the heartbeat, you know.*

Cameron: *Disgusting. True. See you there.*

They finished their drinks. Outside, morning had become more morning—that second wave when the post arrives and the light stops apologising. Joshua walked him as far as the corner where the pavement chooses two ways and pretends the choice matters more than it does.

“So now what?” Joshua asked.

Michael grinned, which is a kind of amen in draft form.

“Now we live the day,” he said. “And then we sit down and say the whole thing plain—napkin, brothers, empty chair, cold floor on day one—and we let the last line do the carrying.”

“You already know the last line,” Joshua said.

“I’ve always known it,” Michael said. “I just had to become someone who could mean it.”

He went home, washed a plate, answered an email, stood in the doorway of his own life without demanding a hatch. The monogram did not glow. It did not need to. It had entered the quieter office of true things: not spectacle, furniture—something you walk past and still live under.

The rest of us were waking in a thousand kitchens.

Including, if you are reading this, the particular skull you cannot leave—

which was never exile.

Which was always the only room.

Which was, and is, about to be told a joke serious enough to build a world on.

He set two mugs out for tomorrow without knowing who would use the second—Joshua, Cameron, or only the idea of company. Hospitality toward the waking. Not a cult. A table with steam rising and an empty chair that was not emptiness, only space left on purpose.

Then he slept inside the only room, which had always been large enough for the rest of us.
