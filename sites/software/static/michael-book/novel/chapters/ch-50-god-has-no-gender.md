# Chapter 50
## God Has No Gender

“If I say this badly,” Michael said, “people will hear a culture war before they hear a claim about the total.”

They were at Joshua’s table. Rain ticked the window like a polite audience. Joshua had unplugged the toaster as a precaution against dramatic gestures; the cord hung limp, a small defeated snake.

Joshua poured coffee. “Say it carefully, then. Bad is optional.”

Michael wrapped both hands around the mug. Heat. Ceramic. The ordinary courage of holding something warm while you try not to lie.

“God has no gender. Not as a fashion statement. As an entailment. If we’re talking about the whole—the field with no outside—then the whole doesn’t need a locker room. Gender is a real pattern inside the field: bodies, languages, roles, wounds, joys. The total isn’t a contestant in that pattern. It *includes* the pattern. It isn’t exhausted by one side of it.”

Joshua nodded once. “And the generative poles?”

“Not cancelled,” Michael said quickly. “I’m not sterilising the cosmology to win a committee vote. Entering and receiving still generate. Making and holding still matter. Those poles show up in male and female lives, and in more lives than the binary can caption, and in non-sexual making too. The mistake is crowning one pole as the True Name of God and calling the other junior, dirty, or merely metaphorical.”

He stopped. Checked Joshua’s face for flinch or cheerleading. Found only attention—brother-attention, which is rarer than agreement.

“I grew up with He,” Michael said. “I’m not burning the pronoun in the street. I’m refusing to let a pronoun become a border fence around the whole. She-language has been medicine for people injured by only-He. Neither medicine is the same as saying the total is a bloke with a beard who happens to own infinity.”

Joshua smiled. “Bookstore version?”

Michael tried. “God isn’t a boy or a girl. People are. God is the room they’re all in—including the arguments about the room.”

“Ship it,” Joshua said. “Then let Cameron kick the tyres.”

Afternoon: Michael walked past the community hall. Lights off. Doorway still a doorway. Inside, on other days, children learned words for themselves while adults outside fought over which words God was allowed. The fight mattered because people matter. The fight also sometimes forgot the scale of the claim—as if infinity needed a changing-room assignment before it could keep the rain ticking.

He sat on a low wall. A van advertised a gym with a jawline. A bus shelter sold perfume with a body. Culture was not shy about gendering desire. Michael did not need the total to join the adverts.

Cameron arrived on a bicycle that had seen better decades, helmet slightly crooked, eyes already doing tyre-kick.

“Motte,” Cameron said, still astride, “or I turn around.”

“Motte: don’t reduce infinity to a mascot,” Michael said. “Bailey I refuse: therefore my politics are physics. Pronouns are pastoral tools, not ontology crowns. Gender real. Total not on a team.”

Cameron considered, rain beading on the helmet. “Acceptable. Grudgingly. As is tradition.” He pointed a gloved finger. “If you use this chapter to erase anyone’s body as a problem for God, I will end you with kindness and a flowchart.”

“Bodies stay,” Michael said. “Poles stay. Crowns fail.”

“Then ride,” Cameron said, and pedalled off before the conversation could become a podcast.

Joshua found Michael still on the wall ten minutes later. “You’ve got the look.”

“What look?”

“The look of a man who nearly started two fights in his head and chose brickwork instead.”

“Growth,” Michael said dryly.

They watched traffic. Michael thought of the wolf week—alpha as mum and dad, both, pack care; omega not a sacred scapegoat job. Family under hierarchy cosplay. Generative under ownership cosplay. Practice for this sentence.

“You’re allowed to feel tender about it,” Joshua said. “People have been harmed by gendered God-talk. People have also been harmed by being told their bodies are a theological problem. Both wounds are in the field.”

“No outside to put them in,” Michael said.

“Exactly. So be gentle and precise. Not mushy. Not cruel.”

Evening, at home, Michael tested the claim against faces he loved—people for whom He-language had been home, people for whom it had been a bruise, people who lived between and beyond the old binary captions. The claim was not that their stories stopped mattering. The claim was that infinity did not need to pick a changing-room to keep generating the world.

He slept better. The heat of the coming monogram week was already in the hall—polite, patient, not yet named.

It had somewhere honest to sit: beside respect, not on top of it.
