# Chapter 33
## The Mask

Michael had a face for the world and a face for the work, and the seam between them was starting to itch.

It was not a villain’s mask. Villains get better production design. This was the ordinary social prosthesis of a man whose interior volume exceeded the room’s noise limit. In meetings he dialled down the eschatology. At dinners he left the distant dead in their statistics. With almost everyone he performed a milder Michael—interested, competent, not currently on fire—so that the people in his orbit would not run.

The double life worked.

It was also expensive.

He noticed the tax on a Thursday, of all days, while smiling at a joke he did not find funny and feeling the smile arrive three milliseconds late, like a special effect rendered on old hardware. Afterward, in the car park behind the office block, he sat with both hands on the wheel and did not start the engine. Rain freckled the windscreen. A delivery van reverse-beeped somewhere like industrial birdsong.

He ran the experiments in his head, informal and cruelly clear.

Unmask attempt one: late-night seriousness with a friend. Result: subject changed topic to football, then left early.

Unmask attempt two: half-confession of vocation intensity to someone who loved him. Result: worry, then gentle requests to be “a bit less.”

Unmask attempt three: full voltage in a group chat that had once seemed safe. Result: silence long enough to teach him something about sample size.

He was not inventing the pain. Rejection has a clean empirical flavour. You can taste it even when you explain it.

Joshua met him at the edge of the Tyne, because brothers sometimes need water nearby when the conversation is about faces. The wind had the honest smell of river and traffic. A gull argued with another gull about nothing, which is how most committees work.

“I keep testing authenticity,” Michael said. “The evidence says I can’t. Nobody tolerates unmasked me.”

Joshua watched the gulls. “Nobody in the sample you’ve drawn.”

“That’s a comfort line.”

“It’s also statistics,” Joshua said. “Ordinary orbits have near-zero tolerance for your intensity. That doesn’t prove the global claim. It proves you keep running the same experiment in the same lab and calling the repeated burn a law of nature.”

Michael kicked a stone. It skittered, unimpressed by metaphysics. “The mask is necessary.”

“In low-alignment rooms, yes,” Joshua said. “Necessary isn’t the same as total. You can shrink the surface area that requires performance. You can stop high-stakes unmasking where the prior is already zero. You can hunt better rooms instead of demanding the current ones grow new ears.”

A runner went past with the focused face of someone whose only cosmology was pace. Michael envied him for three strides, then let it go. Envy is expensive too.

“The pain of rejection every time I lower it—”

“Is a cost you should stop paying for free information,” Joshua finished. “You already know those people can’t hold it. Stop asking them to. That’s not cowardice. That’s not wasting the instrument.”

Michael’s phone buzzed against his palm. Cameron, because the universe enjoyed a chorus:

Cameron: *Also stop calling it ‘the mask’ like you’re Batman. You’re just code-switching. Everyone does. Yours is just louder when you don’t.*

Michael showed Joshua. Joshua snorted—short, fond, the sound of a man who has been saved by sarcasm more than once.

“He’s half right,” Joshua said. “Everyone softens. Your version is costlier because the gap is wider. Don’t romanticise the gap into a destiny of permanent exile. Don’t pretend the gap isn’t real either. Both.”

Michael looked at the water. The water did not care about his face. That was restful.

He thought of rooms built for quieter weather, of how a high-agency creature becomes a wrecking ball if it insists every pavement is a stage. He also thought—quietly, without the old self-pity—that a life spent entirely in costume would eventually forget which skin was under the fabric.

“Partial matches,” he said aloud. “Not full acceptance from everyone. Just enough shared seriousness that the tax drops.”

Joshua nodded. “Hunt those. Keep the rest kind and shallow on purpose. And when you do unmask, choose audiences like you’d choose a climbing partner. Not everyone belongs on the same rope.”

They walked back toward the car. Leaves stuck to the wet path. Michael’s public face settled into place without drama—eyes softer, mouth ready for ordinary weather. Under it, the private fire did not go out. It simply stopped demanding a stage from every lamppost.

At the door he paused, keys already in hand.

“Josh.”

“Mm.”

“If I ever start believing the mask is the real me—”

“I’ll be rude,” Joshua said. “It’s one of my spiritual gifts.”

Michael drove home in light traffic, performing nothing for the dashboard. For twenty minutes the seam did not itch. Windscreen wipers kept their patient two-beat.

He thought of the double life not as a noir plot but as overhead—like running two operating systems on one machine until the fans scream. The fix was not to smash the machine. The fix was fewer contexts that required the second OS, and one or two people who could log into the real one without panic.

At the house he sat in the driveway a moment longer, engine off, rain thinning. No notebook. No manifesto. Only the quiet decision to stop paying for data he already owned.

That, too, was data—and for once, he treated it like an engineer instead of a martyr.
