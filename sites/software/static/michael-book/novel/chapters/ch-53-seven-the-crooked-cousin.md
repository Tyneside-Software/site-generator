# Chapter 53
## Seven The Crooked Cousin

Seven arrived late to the party and rearranged the snacks.

Michael noticed it on the keyboard first—the old adjacency joke, six and seven, caret and ampersand, both and not-both—then in the week’s weather: seven messages he had postponed, seven steps from bed to kettle without thinking, a seven-year-old on the street counting loudly as if numbers were a form of flight.

He recorded a voice note for Joshua while walking, coat collar up against a wind that had opinions:

“Seven’s the crooked cousin. Completion number in one language. Rest day. Luck in another key. And if eight gets to stand sideways and look rude, seven has been doing a related cartoon for years—hooked, leaning, the poor man’s punchline. Still clothed. Mapping relatives. Not hosting the hen do yet.”

Joshua’s reply came as a text while Michael stood in the corner shop choosing milk with the gravity of a man who has learned that ordinary errands keep cosmology from becoming pure theatre: *Family reunion of dirty doodles. Still clothed? Good. Buy actual milk.*

He bought milk. The receipt total was ordinary. He refused to force it into seven anything.

At home he drew 7 and 8 side by side like siblings at a wedding who do not share politics but share cheekbones. Seven: a cliff with a diving board. Eight: a racetrack. Both could be harnessed by a teenage mind into body jokes. Culture had already done that work in toilet stalls from Corinth to Gateshead.

“Why bother, then?” he asked the empty chair.

The empty chair, trained by now, seemed to answer in Joshua’s register: *Because the chain is about what the sacred shares with the ordinary, including the toilet-stall brain. Not to degrade the sacred. To stop pretending the sacred is allergic to the body that writes icons.*

Joshua arrived at six with fish and chips because some theologies require salt and vinegar. They ate from paper on the kitchen table like students again, grease blooming through the wrapping in soft translucent maps.

“Seven completes,” Joshua said between chips. “Eight restarts. That’s a cleaner axis than the doodles, and it still fits generativity. Rest, then go. Sabbath, then world. Death-and-rest stories, then whatever you call the morning after.”

“I like that better as load-bearing,” Michael said. “Doodles as stained glass, not foundation.”

“Both can be true if you keep the labels.” Joshua pointed a chip at him. “You’re doing well. I can feel you wanting to sprint to the monogram.”

“I do.” Michael admitted it like a fact that no longer needed a landfill. “The letters are loud in my head. Fonts. Spans. The napkin that doesn’t exist yet.”

“Soon,” Joshua said. “Delta first, maybe. Tips before shafts, if we’re being botanical.”

Michael groaned. Joshua looked pleased, which is a brother’s ancient right.

They walked the paper to the bin together. On the path Michael counted seven fence posts before a broken one—the crooked cousin in timber. He did not photograph it. He let it be a fence. The city was full of sevens that did not need him: bus routes, shirt numbers, the seventh attempt at a parking manoeuvre across the street. He practised non-annexation like a sport.

Back inside, the kitchen still smelled of vinegar and victory. Michael washed his hands and tapped the 7 key and the 8 key lightly, a pianist checking a chord without playing the concerto.

“Not yet,” he told them.

The keys, being plastic, did not mind waiting.

He thought of rest days that had saved him and rest days he had wasted performing busyness; of stories that end on the seventh beat so the eighth can feel like mercy. The doodle-cousins could wait in the hall. The rhythm of stop-and-go was already theology enough for one fish-and-chip evening.

Cameron, when shown the sketch later: *Your inventory is becoming a red-light district of numerals.*

Michael: *It’s a census.*

Cameron: *Call it what you like. Sunglasses on for the next chapter.*

Joshua texted from home: *Don’t sprint in your sleep.*

Michael: *Walking pace. Crooked cousins on a lead.*

Joshua: *Good. Delta tomorrow. Keep the labels on.*

Michael left the sketch on the table under a clean plate. Not framed. Not hidden. Just present—like a relative you have introduced properly, so nobody has to pretend they don’t know who he is when he finally speaks.

Somewhere the field—which has room for sunglasses, sacraments, salt, and vinegar—continued its crooked, lucky, unfinished count.
