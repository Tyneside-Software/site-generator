# Chapter 26
## The Ordinary Answer

Tuesday, Michael reread Adams for the joke and found a plank in his own eye.

The morning after meeting Cameron’s different speeds still hummed in him—kindness as pace-setting, forty-two as seatbelt. He wanted to honour that without turning it into a new superiority: *look how humble I am with my ordinary answer*. Humility that performs is just pride in a towel.

He had owned *The Hitchhiker’s Guide* since an age when towels seemed like sufficient preparation for adulthood. Forty-two had been dorm-room scripture: the Answer to Life, the Universe, and Everything, comic because it was mundane, holy because it refused grandeur. Cameron lived there still, cheerfully, like a man who had found a good flat and stopped house-hunting.

Michael, post-crisis, wanted both the joke and the next corridor.

They met in the park this time, where ordinary answers grew on trees in the form of dogs and weather. A woman taught a child to ride a bike: fall, laugh, try again—the oldest curriculum. Cameron tossed Michael a packet of crisps as if theology required salt.

‘Forty-two,’ Cameron said. ‘Before you invent a religion around punctuation.’

‘I’m not—’ Michael began, then stopped, because he was always slightly in danger of doing exactly that. ‘I’m trying to keep the ordinary answer *and* the deeper climb. Both.’

‘Both is your brand,’ Cameron said. ‘Just don’t use both as a way to never land.’

Michael opened the crisps. Salt did theology in its own way. ‘There’s a verse. Luke. Something about a plank and a speck. Brother’s eye. Judge not, or at least judge after optometry.’

Cameron squinted. ‘You’re going to moralise my forty-two?’

‘I’m going to moralise *me*,’ Michael said. ‘I spent a night terrified of layers and half-proud of seeing further than people who sleep. That’s a plank. Your ordinary number might be wisdom. My speed might be anxiety in a nice coat.’

Cameron was quiet, which was rarer than his jokes. He watched the bike wobble and recover. ‘Alright. That’s fair. I can be smug about not overthinking. That’s a speck that thinks it’s a style.’

Michael pulled out his phone and read the verse quietly, not as performance—Luke 6:42, the comedy of hypocrisy with carpentry props. *How can you say, Friend, let me take the speck out of your eye, when you yourself fail to see the plank in your own?*

‘Adams and Luke,’ he said. ‘Same department. The answer that punctures pomposity. Forty-two as comic deflation. Plank as moral deflation. Both telling me to stop recruiting friends into my intensity parade.’

Cameron stretched his legs on the damp grass and immediately regretted it, which was also ordinary. ‘I like being sandpaper. I don’t like being a project. If forty-two helps you check your eye, use it. If you start preaching forty-two at people who need a hospital, I’ll revoke your towel privileges.’

‘Deal.’

They walked as far as the coffee van. Cameron bought; Michael carried the cups because hands need jobs when pride is being dismantled. Steam rose into cold air and vanished the way steam always does—without a moral.

Joshua appeared on a call when Michael rang him from the path, walking while Cameron queued for sugar he did not need.

‘Plank first,’ Joshua said after the summary. ‘Always. The ordinary answer isn’t the enemy of depth. It’s the enemy of spiritual showing off.’

‘Amen,’ Michael said.

‘And Cameron?’

‘Still at forty-two. Kind about it. Threatened me with towels.’

‘Good man,’ Joshua said. ‘Keep him. Speed without witnesses becomes a private cult.’

That afternoon Michael did something aggressively ordinary: food shop, bin night, a text to someone he had neglected while chasing hatches. Each act felt like removing sawdust from his own vision. Not glamorous. Possibly the point.

The neglected friend replied with a photo of a child holding a stick like a sword, captioned only *training*. Michael laughed until his ribs hurt. Other people’s lives had continued without his ontology. That was not an insult. That was the plank dissolving into good news.

In the supermarket aisle he watched a couple argue gently about biscuits—which pack, which offer, which future self would be grateful. It was Luke and Adams in crumb form: the speck of preference, the plank of who decides, the ordinary answer of *get both if we can afford it*. They got both. Michael saluted them with a nod they did not see.

He wrote in the notebook:

**42 = the joke that stops the cathedral mid-brick when the cathedral is only ego.**  
**Luke 6:42 = same joke with higher stakes.**  
**Check own eye before offering friends laser surgery.**

Evening, he made tea from supermarket own-brand bags and drank it without irony. Cameron rang once, brief as a blessing.

‘Ordinary answer, beverage form,’ Michael said.

‘Blessed are the own-brand,’ Cameron replied, ‘for they shall inherit the cupboard. Now floss. Horizontal beam includes gums. Joshua’s orders—he copied me in like a weapon.’

Michael slept easier than the night of layers. Not because the layers had vanished—because he had stopped confusing depth with superiority. The abyss could wait its turn behind bin night. The journey could be enjoyed without a podium.

The ordinary answer was not the end of the book.

It was the bit where the hero realises he is also the fool, and buys milk.
