# Chapter 73
## Theory Of Mind Before Genesis

Cameron insisted on the pub with the sticky menus, on the grounds that theology improved when surrounded by people arguing about football and the referee’s eyesight.

‘You’re going to talk about God again,’ he said, once the pints arrived and the table had accepted its fate as a coaster museum. ‘Before you do, demonstrate that you believe other minds exist.’

Michael blinked. ‘I what?’

‘Theory of mind,’ Cameron said. ‘Basic kit. The thing toddlers learn between tantrums: other people have thoughts that are not your thoughts. Religions are full of adults who flunked the toddler exam and compensated with scripture, architecture, and a tone of voice.’

Joshua, present and amused, raised his glass. Foam caught the light. ‘He’s not wrong. Painful, but not wrong.’

Michael thought of R.’s red lines—said out loud, not fog—of the bookshop window years ago in spirit if not calendar, of every time he had treated a face as a mirror for his chain and called the reflection dialogue.

‘Say more,’ he said, which is how you pass the exam mid-sitting instead of failing with confidence.

Cameron tore a beer mat into careful strips, as if precision might keep the universe from collapsing into monologue. ‘You meet someone. Your brain writes a novel about them in three seconds: motives, fears, secret agreement with your thesis. Then you argue with the novel. The person is elsewhere, buying crisps. That is not faith. That is bad animation. That is you puppet-showing your way through a friendship.’

‘And Genesis?’ Michael asked.

‘Genesis can wait,’ Cameron said. ‘Cosmology can wait. Your monogram can wait. If you can’t model *she is not thinking what I would think in a headscarf*, you have no business at a Scripture Reasoning table. You’ll just hold a puppet show and call it dialogue, and everyone will be too polite to call security.’

Joshua nodded. ‘Before creation stories, creation of space for another subject. Horizontal beam as cognitive science. Love as refusing to fake the data.’

Michael took a long drink. The football crowd roared at a near-miss; someone’s theory of the referee’s mind was unprintable and confidently wrong. The air smelled of chips and the ancient optimism of Saturday.

‘R. is not my continuity DLC,’ Michael said. ‘I’ve said it. I need practices, not slogans. Slogans are how I fail the exam while sounding like I sat it.’

Cameron pointed a beer-mat strip at him like a wand that refused magic on principle. ‘Practice one: before you answer, summarise her view until she says *yes, that*. Not *yes, close enough for my system*. Practice two: notice when you’re bored by her specifics because they don’t feed your pattern. Boredom is a tell. It means you wanted a mirror and got a person. Practice three: ask what would falsify your reading of her—then accept an answer that isn’t your monogram wearing a different hat.’

‘That’s… adult,’ Michael said.

‘Forty-two is adult,’ Cameron said. ‘You’ve been doing fireworks. Fireworks are pretty. They make terrible conversation partners. They also set curtains on fire if you’re not careful.’

Joshua leaned in, brother-voice under the noise. ‘Also practice four from the brother department: when you feel the thrill of *she almost agrees*, check whether you have stopped seeing her. Thrill is not evidence. Thrill is your nervous system throwing a party for itself.’

Michael put the four practices into his phone, thumb clumsy with sincerity, the note titled simply enough that he might actually open it later. A man at the next table began explaining to his date, at volume, what she really felt about her job. The date’s smile became a diplomatic incident; her eyes did the quiet work of someone updating their exit plan.

Cameron flicked his gaze that way and back.

‘Exhibit A,’ he said gently. ‘Theory of mind failure with free peanuts.’

Michael apologised to the air for his whole species, which the air accepted without comment.

They ate chips. They talked about work, about a film that had lied about London’s geography, about whether the free kick had been deserved. Only near the end, when the pints were lower and the football had settled into mutual grievance, did Michael risk a sentence about God.

‘If the field is one,’ he said, ‘other minds aren’t outside it. They’re the field being someone else. Theory of mind isn’t a barrier to unity. It’s how unity stops being a slogan that erases people and starts being something you can sit next to without turning them into furniture.’

Cameron considered, chewing, then granted a rare inch. ‘That version I can drink to. The version where unity means everyone is secretly you with worse taste—I will leave the pub, and I will take the crisps.’

‘Deal.’

Outside, under a sky that contained every unshared thought in the city, Michael stood on the pavement and did not compose a thesis. He texted R. nothing profound—only *Hope your day was human*—and did not attach a diagram, a vow, or a clever reading of her silence.

She replied with a photo of a cat on a wall, captioned: *Theory of cat: you exist for warm laps. Other theories pending.*

He laughed on the pavement, alone and corrected, the sound small against traffic. Joshua clapped his shoulder once. Cameron checked the time like a man who still had a life that was not this conversation.

Before Genesis, there was this: the hard, comic work of remembering that the person across the table was not a draft of himself—and that love, like good science, begins by not faking the data of another mind, even when the data refuses to fit the monogram, even when the beer mat is in pieces, even when the football is wrong and the referee is only human.

Michael put his phone away. The cat on the wall, miles away, continued not to care about his ontology.

That, too, was evidence of other minds.
