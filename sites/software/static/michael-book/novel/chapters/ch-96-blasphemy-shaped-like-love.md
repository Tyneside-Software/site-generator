# Chapter 96
## Blasphemy Shaped Like Love

The monogram still offended people. That was data, not a marketing plan.

Michael discovered it again when a man at a dinner—friend of a friend, confident wine, the sort of confidence that owns a holiday home in his opinions—heard a careful version of α==ω and went grey, then loud. ‘That’s blasphemy. You’re sexualising God.’

The table froze in the British way: eyes on roast potatoes, cutlery paused mid-air as if etiquette could reverse time. Someone coughed into a napkin. The host smiled the smile of people who regret their guest list. Candles did their small orange work of making everyone look kinder than the conversation.

Michael set his fork down. Heart rate up. The old hunger for a microphone and a martyr’s halo reached for the room. He breathed. He saw R. across the candles, not a prop—a friend with her own red lines, who had not come to be evidence. He saw Joshua’s small nod: *careful, not cowardly*.

‘It might be blasphemy in your court,’ he said. ‘In mine it’s a claim that generation isn’t a dirty annex God outsources to a shame department. I’m not asking you to print it on a youth-group hoodie. I’m saying the joke landed as love for me—love of bodies, love of the whole, love that refuses a pure upstairs where sex is other people’s problem. If that still sounds like filth only, we may be stuck. I’ll live with stuck. I won’t live with sneering at your grandmother to feel free.’

‘Love,’ the man said, sceptical as a closed gate. ‘Convenient word. Everything is love until the vicar leaves.’

R., present because the host collected interesting combinations of humans the way some people collect cookbooks, spoke before Michael could dig a cleverer hole. ‘Some blasphemy is just cruelty wearing a mask of freedom. Some “blasphemy” is tenderness that refuses a cruel purity. Discernment. I don’t share his monogram. I have watched it make him less contemptuous of flesh, including his own. That shape I will call love-adjacent. If it makes him sneer at your grandmother’s piety, it has flipped into something else—and I will say so to his face, not behind a potato.’

Joshua, across the table, nodded. ‘Shame into love was my family’s homework. If the scandal doesn’t produce care, it’s cosplay. Cosplay with incense is still cosplay. I have worn the costume. It photographs well. It fails at three in the morning when someone needs a lift to hospital.’

The man still disliked it. He was allowed. Dislike is not a crime; contempt can be. Dinner continued with a heroic pivot to weather, then to a football score that let everyone breathe. Michael passed the gravy. Gravy is underrated diplomacy. The potatoes, blameless, continued to be potatoes.

Later, washing up with the host’s too-small towel in a kitchen that smelled of fat and relief, Michael said to R. and Joshua in the steam: ‘I needed that phrase. Blasphemy shaped like love. Also: love shaped like blasphemy to systems that need disgust to run. I don’t want to become a professional offender. I also don’t want to sand the joke into a pebble that never cuts anything false.’

R. handed him a plate. ‘Don’t fall in love with being offensive. That’s ego’s favourite coat—looks brave, warms only you. Fall in love with being accurate and kind at the same time. If accuracy looks like scandal, bear the cost without recruiting martyrs. Martyrs at dinner parties are exhausting. They make the host refill water as penance.’

Joshua stacked glasses with the care of a man who had broken one last Christmas and still owed a story about it. ‘Also: if you leave needing them to admit you were holy, you were hunting with a halo. Same sin as missionary eggshells, different cutlery.’

Cameron, informed by text while Michael dried, replied: *If your “love” needs a victim to feel real, it’s not love. If their “reverence” needs a scapegoat body to feel clean, it’s not reverence. Both fail. Potatoes are innocent. Forty-two.*

Michael showed the others. Steam curled around the phone like stage smoke, unearned. Joshua snorted. ‘Cameron just saved Christianity and decency in one text. Don’t tell him. He’ll demand chips as tithe.’

Walking home, Harriet linked her arm through his. Streetlights made wet pavement into cheap glitter. A fox—possibly the same unlisted fox, possibly a cousin—crossed the road without consulting anyone’s theology.

‘You didn’t perform,’ Harriet said. ‘You also didn’t collapse. Proud. Now tell me privately if you’re okay—not the theology, the body.’

He was shaky and clear. Adrenaline leaving like a bad guest who had overstayed the coffee. ‘I keep wanting everyone to laugh with the napkin. Some people will always hear only filth. I can’t control their ears. I can control whether I use the scandal to feel superior. Superior is the real blasphemy if anything is—standing outside the only room sneering.’

‘That last bit,’ Harriet said. ‘Tattoo it on your manners. Not on your skin. Manners last longer if you practise. Skin fades. Manners, if you keep them, don’t.’

At home he took out both napkins—monogram and bigger-book—and did not add a third. He sat on the bed edge until the adrenaline left, Harriet’s hand on his back, no speech required. The house held them the way mattresses hold sleepers: without demanding a creed first.

Blasphemy shaped like love: possible.

Love shaped like a weapon: also possible.

The work was telling them apart at dinner, with potatoes listening—and keeping the coat warm without picking anyone’s pocket.
