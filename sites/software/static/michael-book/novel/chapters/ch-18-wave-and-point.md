# Chapter 18
## Wave And Point

Wednesday night the pub smelled of wet wool and ambition. Serious questions arrived, ordered pints, and became bets before last orders.

Joshua drew on a beer mat with a biro that had already lived a full life. Two figures: one a scribble of ripples, the other a hard little dot.

'Wave,' he said, tapping the ripples. 'Point.' The dot. 'Same light. Depends how you ask.'

Michael frowned into mild that was already losing its head. 'You're explaining quantum mechanics with a wet mat and a hangover of school physics.'

'I'm explaining a fight people have been having since they learned to measure,' Joshua said. 'Wave or particle? The universe's answer is: stop forcing me into a team shirt.'

At the next table a man insisted football was simple. His friend insisted it was not. Above them, the ceiling fans turned without joining either theory of offside.

Michael remembered double-slit diagrams, chalk dust, teachers who brightened when confusion spread. He also remembered the adult version: threads where wave and particle became personality types, as if photons held opinions and voted.

'Both,' he said.

'Both,' Joshua agreed. 'Not mush. Not "whatever you feel." Both as in: the experiment you run is part of the answer you get. Ask wave-questions, get wave-behaviour. Ask point-questions, get points. Light does not owe you one costume for every party.'

Michael turned the mat. The ripples looked like a headache sketched by a tired artist. The dot looked like a full stop with ambition.

'People hate this,' he said. 'They want the thing-in-itself to stand still while they walk around it with a clipboard.'

'And the thing-in-itself keeps dancing with the clipboard,' Joshua said. 'Deep or cheeky. I vote both.'

They made it a bet, because brothers improve philosophy with stakes. Joshua bet that before the night ended someone would use *literally* to mean *almost*. Michael bet the fruit machine would eat a pound without apology. Both won. The universe declined a single jersey again.

Michael thought of the Both/And shop they had joked into existence—beer-mat signage, open late, closed to false dilemmas. Wave and point belonged on the shelf next to free and caused, sacred and ordinary. Not slogans. Things that *happened* when you looked carefully and refused to amputate half the evidence.

'What about people?' he asked. 'Waves or points?'

Joshua wiped condensation with a thumb. 'Points who make waves. Or waves that localise long enough to buy a round. Depends whether you're counting births or noticing moods across a city.'

'That's not physics.'

'It's the same allergy to either/or,' Joshua said. 'Physics just has better graphs.'

At the bar a woman laughed too loud at a joke that might not have been funny. The laugh rolled outward—a social wave—then collapsed into her particular face: point of embarrassment and joy. Michael watched the conversion, then named it, because he was becoming the sort of man who named things in pubs.

'Wave and point,' he said softly.

Joshua raised his glass. 'To light that won't sit for a single portrait.'

They drank. Outside, rain complicated the streetlamps—spread, sparkle, puddle-mirror—each photon allegedly forced to choose a résumé and somehow still lighting the pavement for everyone.

Walking home, Michael passed a window where a politician chose a side with theatrical courage. The crowd waved. The man pointed. Wave and point, badly cast. An advert promised certainty in a world made of probabilities. Michael almost saluted the irony and kept walking, which was the more British response.

A fox crossed the road with the economy of a creature that had never filled a false dilemma on a form. Headlights made it a brief particle of gold; the hedge took it back as a ripple in the dark.

At home he stuck the beer mat to the fridge with a strawberry magnet. The mat would not survive the month. The idea might. Fridge light on his shoes, he thought of every argument he had lost by insisting a person be only one thing: only strong, only kind, only wrong, only finished. People, like light, had the bad manners to answer the experiment you actually ran.

He slept without solving light. Light did not require his solution. It required his willingness to stop demanding a simpler universe than the one that paid for the streetlamps—and rarer still, simpler friends than the ones who loved him.

Thursday would bring empty space and invoices. Tonight had been enough: two brothers, one mat, a bet the universe settled by being itself in public.

Wave.

And point.

Not or.
