# Chapter 75
## Read — Will You Not Think

The phrase arrived on a Tuesday like a pebble in a shoe: useful, annoying, impossible to ignore.

R. had said it lightly over voice note while Michael was between meetings—*There’s a refrain people forget when they paint us as anti-reason: read, and will you not think?*—and then she had gone back to her day, leaving him with the pebble and a calendar full of people who wanted answers faster than reading allows. Speed, he was learning, is often a costume for fear.

He looked it up properly that evening, not via the first rage-blog, but through the careful path: translation notes, context, the way a living tradition hears its own book. Joshua sat at the other end of the sofa with a laptop, half working, half available as amen service. The cat of temporary custody judged them both for not being warm enough, which is a rigorous philosophy if you are a cat.

‘It’s not a Western import,’ Michael said, more to himself. ‘The demand to think. It’s inside the grain. I had a silhouette of Islam as off-switch. The silhouette was lazy. Laziness is also a kind of blasphemy if anything is—refusing the work of a face.’

‘Shocking,’ Joshua said mildly, not looking up. ‘Almost as if millions of people aren’t cartoon extras in your recognition week.’

Michael grimaced. ‘I’ve met the cartoons in my own head. The lazy story: faith as off-switch. My week could have become a new off-switch—*I’ve seen the joke, stop reading*. This line won’t let me. It turns the mirror.’

He played R.’s note again. Her voice carried affection and steel. She wasn’t recruiting him into Islam. She was correcting a silhouette the way you correct a friend’s posture—kind, firm, slightly humiliating if you have pride.

Cameron, when pinged, replied: *If a tradition tells you to think, and you use that sentence only to win arguments against it, you failed the instruction. Apply thinking to your own napkin too. Also to your emails. Thinking is not only for gods.*

‘Sandpaper delivered,’ Michael told Joshua.

‘Good grit,’ Joshua said. ‘Use it on yourself first. Always yourself first. That’s the part people skip so they can sand their neighbours.’

Michael made tea and did the instruction in the smallest way available to a man with a Tuesday: he read a page he usually skimmed—work notes full of names he treated as furniture; a friend’s long message he had planned to answer with a heart emoji; a paragraph of physics popularisation he had been faking comprehension of at dinner. He read as if someone might ask him what it said, not what it reminded him of.

He wrote three honest sentences about the physics paragraph and felt the shame of prior bluffing. The shame was clean. Humiliation, properly used, is a tutor.

R. came by later with a return of a borrowed umbrella and stayed for ten minutes in the doorway, the way busy friends do—body half in, half out, trust still full.

‘You left me a pebble,’ Michael said.

‘Good. Walk on it.’ She leaned on the frame. ‘I get tired of being told my book is pure submission in the cartoon sense—mindless kneeling. Submission in my life includes the terror and joy of having to understand. Of being addressed as someone who can. *Will you not think* is not flattery. It’s a demand. Demands honour you. Petting does not.’

Michael held the umbrella out; she waved it off—*keep it, weather’s not done*. Rain ticked the landing window. ‘I’m applying it to my bigger book,’ he said. ‘Not as a quote to annex. As a mirror. Where have I stopped reading because I liked my summary? Where have I used your sentence as furniture in my room?’

‘Everywhere, if you’re like the rest of us,’ R. said. ‘Do it anyway. And don’t put me in the acknowledgements as proof you think. Just think.’

After she left, Michael opened the notebook and wrote the line in English, then left a blank line beneath it—not filled with his monogram, not filled with rebuttal, not filled with a clever both-and that would make the blank feel like a failure.

**Read.**  
**Will you not think?**  
**________________**

Joshua looked over, laptop finally shut. The cat relocated to his lap with the arrogance of creatures who know they are the point. ‘You’re not going to complete that with α==ω?’

‘Not tonight,’ Michael said. ‘Tonight the blank is the thinking. The monogram can wait its turn like everyone else. Even revelations queue a queue if they’re serious about not becoming tyrants.’

They sat in the lamp light. Outside, rain tested the umbrella’s future on the peg. Inside, a man who had recently seen a cosmic joke practised the older joke of attention: that the universe keeps writing, and the least you can do is move your eyes across the page without deciding you already know the ending.

Especially when a friend has trusted you with a sentence from her home, and asked only that you not turn it into furniture in yours—not a trophy, not a badge, not a line in a diary that proves you are broad-minded while your eyes stay closed.

Michael closed the notebook on the blank.

Thinking, for once, included knowing when to stop writing over someone else’s ink—and leaving the white space white, which is harder than filling it, and more faithful to the demand that had walked in on a Tuesday and refused to leave quietly.
