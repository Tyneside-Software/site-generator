# Chapter 39
## Romans, Efficient And Ashamed

They met by the river again because some conversations need moving water to dilute them.

Michael had been thinking about crosses—not as jewellery, not as tribal badge, but as an object with a job description. Rome had been good at job descriptions. Roads. Law. Nails.

The path was wet from earlier rain. Gulls conducted committee meetings overhead. A rower slid past, neat as a sentence with good punctuation.

“I keep coming back to the engineering,” Michael said. “Vertical and horizontal. We already did God and neighbour. We already did the popular sermon with both beams. But the Roman version—”

Joshua waited. He was good at waiting when the sentence needed a longer runway.

“Efficient and ashamed,” Michael said. “That’s the product design. Death on the up-down axis—body failing in public gravity. Shame on the across axis—eyes, crowds, the social kill. One device, two punishments, no spare parts. Brutally optimised.”

Joshua’s face did the thing it did when theology arrived wearing work boots. “Go on.”

“I don’t need secret Roman mystics,” Michael said quickly. “I’m not claiming a lost committee memo from Golgotha Products Ltd. I’m saying: if you design a killing tool that also maximises humiliation, you have built both/and into the wood. Kill and display. End a life and unmake a face. Vertical dead. Horizontal watched.”

Joshua picked up a flat stone and did not throw it yet. Thumb tested the edge. “Connection inverted into disconnect. That’s what I keep hearing in it. The cross as public unbinding—person peeled off from community by spectacle. Then the later move: someone wears the shame and turns the whole apparatus into a sign of love that refuses to stay private.”

“Reinvention,” Michael said.

“Reinvention,” Joshua agreed. “Almost an inversion of the inversion. Shame into shamelessly public love. Not because pain is pretty. Because love refused to let empire keep the last authorship of the symbol.”

They stood with that. Wind came off the water, honest and uninterested in conclusions. Michael’s hands were cold in his pockets. The cold helped; abstractions float less when the body files a weather report.

He thought of his own smaller shames—the mask, the recruiter’s polished fork, the nights he had treated tired people as moral failures for having low bandwidth. Public and private humiliations. The horizontal beam was not only ancient Rome. It was every room where eyes decide your worth.

“Does it help?” Joshua asked. “Or does it just hurt cleverly?”

“Both,” Michael said. “It helps because the both/and isn’t mush here. You can feel the load on each beam. Miss the death and you get sentiment. Miss the shame and you get a private spirituality that never risks being seen. The whole shape needs both, or it’s just a stick.”

Joshua finally skimmed the stone. It hopped twice, then surrendered to physics with a small, undramatic splash. “Church said God and neighbour for years. You sharpened it with Roman product design. I sharpened it with reinvention. Same shop, different shelves.”

Michael’s phone buzzed. Cameron:

Cameron: *If you’re river-side theologising again, bring me into the punchline not the footnotes.*

Michael: *Romans: efficient death + public shame in one tool. Later: love steals the symbol.*

Cameron: *Grim. True enough. Still going to bed at a human hour.*

Michael showed Joshua. Joshua grinned. “Patron sceptic clocks out. Theory improves.”

They walked. The path held joggers and dog-walkers and one man arguing with a headset as if empire had merely changed materials. A child pointed at the rower and received an answer Michael could not hear; the answer looked ordinary, which is a kind of grace.

Michael said, “I used to think the cross was only vertical drama—me and the sky. Then only horizontal ethics—me and the queue. Holding both is harder. Holding death and shame at once is harder still. Holding love rewriting the shame is the bit that doesn’t fit in a spreadsheet.”

“Spreadsheets are bad at resurrection,” Joshua said. “They’re decent at logistics. Rome was logistics. The scandal is what happened to the logistics.”

Michael did not resolve the history into a tidy pamphlet. He did not need to. He had what the hour required: a deeper cut of an old shape—two men by a river, one stone skipping, imperial carpentry too good at its job and then—impossibly—repurposed by a story that refused to let efficiency and shame be the last word.

On the way back to the car he traced a small cross on the misted window—up, across—and wiped it away before it could become a performance.

He thought of the first pass at the beams—God and neighbour, death and shame—and felt this afternoon as a second cut of the same timber. The first time you see a tool you name its parts. The second time you feel what it was built to do to a person.

“Efficient and ashamed,” he said.

“And then,” Joshua said, “something else.”

“The something else is the bit people rush,” Michael said. “I’m not rushing. I want the Roman honesty first. Otherwise the love story becomes wallpaper.”

Joshua nodded. “Hold the shame long enough that the reinvention costs something. Otherwise it’s just a slogan with a plus sign.”

They left the river to its own both/and: fresh and tidal, dirty and alive, going out and coming in under the same bridges.

In the car Michael did not put on music. Silence after a true shape is a kind of respect. Joshua buckled in. Neither filled the quiet with a summary. The wood still hung between them—two beams, one device, and the later theft of authorship by a love that refused to stay private.

The engine started. The river fell behind. The shape did not.
