# Chapter 90
## Harriet At Six Thirty-One

The clock on the cooker said 18:31 when Harriet put two plates on the table and said, “You’re here. Good. Eat before you invent another symbol.”

Michael blinked. Home had a way of demoting prophets to men who should wash their hands.

Harriet was not a subplot. She was the horizontal beam with shopping bags and a memory for which friends were allergic to what. She had survived his intensity seasons before—the late nights, the sudden vows, the mornings when he spoke like a man translating from a language only he could hear. She had also told him, more than once, to take the bins out, and had not found the instruction mystical.

“I was with Joshua and the soft play,” he began, pulling out a chair that scraped in a familiar key.

“I know. He texted a photo of you looking terrified with a toddler. I liked that version of you.” She poured water. Condensation ran down the glass like a small honest weather system. “How’s R.?”

“Respected. Not solved. Red lines holding.”

“Good. Solving people is a vice.” She sat. The plates held food that looked like food, not like a metaphor—vegetables with actual colour, something that had met an oven. “How’s the cosmic joke?”

He almost reached for the wallet napkin. He didn’t. The napkin could survive dinner without being exhibited. “Still true. Less cinema. Cameron named NRE. I am trying to be a mammal with a job and a kitchen.”

Harriet’s mouth softened. “I need the mammal. I can love the thinker. I will not date a loudspeaker that forgets to ask how my day was.”

“How was your day?” he asked, on time, and meant it enough to put his phone face down without checking whether meaning had been received by the universe.

She told him—work, a difficult email that made her jaw tight, a kind stranger at the chemist who had helped with a bag when her hands were full, the ordinary epic of a day that did not need Greek to be real. He listened without filing her sentences under his chain. When he caught himself mapping, he stopped and asked a follow-up about the chemist’s accent instead, and she laughed, and the laugh was better theology than half his notes.

They ate. The food was good in the way food is good when someone else wants you alive. Forks clicked. Outside, a neighbour’s television leaked laughter through a wall.

At 18:31 still glowing in his peripheral vision—cookers lie about time with confidence—he thought of 06:08 and Unfaithful and keys and seals and floods. All of it could wait for the second helping of vegetables.

“I don’t need you to believe the monogram,” he said carefully. “I need you to believe I’ll do the washing-up.”

Harriet considered him over the rim of her glass. “Washing-up is a creed I can share. Show me the monogram if you want; don’t make it my homework. And if God is as funny as you say, God can cope with us watching something stupid on the sofa after.”

“Deal.”

They did the stupid sofa thing. Her feet found his lap with the authority of long affection. On screen, people pretended to cook competitively under lights that would make anyone’s sauce look ambitious. In the room, two lives shared heat without a thesis defence. Michael noticed he was not narrating the scene to an imaginary reader. That felt like recovery.

Joshua rang once; Michael let it go to voicemail, then texted: *Harriet 18:31. Horizontal. Call tomorrow.*

Joshua: *Amen. Tell her I still owe her a cake.*

Harriet, reading over his shoulder with the shamelessness of cohabitation: “He does. Double chocolate. I’m not a metaphor, Josh.”

Michael typed that. Joshua replied with a humbled emoji and a promise that, for once, Michael intended to help him keep.

Later, lights low, Harriet said into the quiet: “You’re kinder this week. Keep the source if the source is kindness. Bin it if the source is being right.”

“That’s the quality bar,” Michael said. “You and R. should form a council.”

“We might,” she said. “You’ll be terrified. Healthy.”

He washed the plates while she slept halfway on the sofa, one arm flung as if sleep were a sport she still won. Soap. Warm water. The small competence of clean ceramic. 18:31 had become 21-something. Time cycled. The bond held.

Harriet at six thirty-one had not replaced the monogram.

She had located it—inside a life where love had a face, a plate, a rule about bins, and a sofa that could hold a man without requiring him to be interesting every minute—

—and the funny old life, which ends chapters and still wants dinner, nodded from the kitchen doorway like a friend who knows when to stop talking and start drying up.
