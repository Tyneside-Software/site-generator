# Chapter 3
## One Room, No Outside

Michael opened the door to the hall.

No wind from another world. No choir. No lighting change of the sort directors favour when meaning arrives. There was a hall: shoes, a radiator that clanked when it felt philosophical, a coat that had hung so long it had acquired opinions and would not be hurried.

He stepped through.

Still here.

Bathroom: tiles, tap, a bottle that promised clarity and delivered soap. Still here.

Bedroom: bed unmade, book face-down, light through half-closed blinds drawing geometry nobody had asked for. Still here.

Cupboard under the sink—because thoroughness is a kind of madness with good paperwork—cleaning products, a cracked bucket, and the same fact wearing rubber gloves. *Here.*

Joshua arrived at half past one with sandwiches and the expression of a man summoned by a text reading only: **doors are lying**.

‘You’re not dead,’ he said, hanging his coat on the opinionated hook. ‘That’s a start. Eat.’

Michael ate half a sandwich standing up, which is how people eat when they are too busy being right about the universe to sit down properly.

‘Every door leads to more of the same room,’ he said. ‘Not the same *room*—I know that. Different wallpaper. Different smells. Never an exit from *being somewhere*. No door out of here-ness. Only doors into other furniture arrangements of here.’

Joshua chewed, mild as milk. ‘People go outside.’

‘Outside the flat. Outside the building. Outside the city. Never outside *outside*. You step through and the air is colder and the sky is bigger and a pigeon judges you, and all of that is still experience, still place, still the same continuous fact wearing a coat.’

They walked to the front door together. Michael opened it onto the street. Grey afternoon. A woman with a shopping trolley negotiating a kerb as if it were a border crossing. The smell of rain that had not quite decided to arrive.

Joshua stepped onto the pavement. Michael followed. They stood like two men who had expected a plot twist and found weather instead.

‘See?’ Michael said. ‘More here. Just larger and slightly damper.’

‘You’re going to be unbearable at Christmas,’ Joshua said, fondly.

They walked as far as the corner shop. Michael bought milk he did not need, because buying milk is what you do when you have just proven something vast and do not want to look unhinged in public. The shop bell tinkled. The man behind the counter nodded. A magazine rack offered celebrity break-ups and tips for a flatter stomach. All of it: here.

On the way back Joshua said, ‘So there’s no outside to the whole thing. No porch of non-being. No waiting room for people who opted out of existence. Unity by default.’

‘Unity sounds religious.’

‘Call it one room with a lot of furniture, then. Call it the field. Call it the only kitchen. I’m not branding it. If every door is an internal door, the building doesn’t have a street. It *is* the street.’

Michael stopped under a tree that was doing its best with the season. A leaf fell with the professional competence of leaves everywhere, landed on the pavement, and became, instantly, part of the same stubborn museum as everything else.

‘When I was a kid,’ he said, ‘I thought space was the outside. Stars. Black. Cold. The bit after the last fence.’

‘And then you grew up and learned space is just a very large cupboard with better marketing.’

‘Worse. Space is still *something*. Vacuum isn’t nothing—it’s a kind of hungry something physicists argue about while drawing diagrams. Even the empty bits file expenses.’

Joshua grinned. ‘You’re going to make vacuum accountants a thing.’

They went back inside. The flat received them without comment, which is what good rooms do. Michael put the milk in the fridge and stood a moment with the door open, looking at the little light, the shelves, the humble republic of jars.

‘One room,’ he said. ‘No outside. Not as a sermon. As a description.’

Joshua washed his hands at the sink, practical as always. ‘Then the next question isn’t “what’s beyond the walls?” It’s “how does the furniture arrange itself?” Maps. Edges. Whether the whole thing has a rim you can fall off.’

Michael closed the fridge. The light went out with a small domestic click that somehow felt like agreement.

He thought of the Chair of Non-Being, still just a chair. He thought of the notebook’s *Start here*. He thought of every door he had opened that day, and how none of them had delivered him to a second universe where the rules were optional.

‘Stay for tea?’ he asked.

‘Always. But this time the tea is food-adjacent. I’m putting bread near it. Call it a meal. Call it a ritual. Call it two brothers in a room with no outside, refusing to starve for philosophy.’

Michael put the kettle on.

Somewhere a bus sighed, and the sound came through the window as if the street and the kitchen had never really been separate rooms at all—only different ends of the same continuous, annoyingly coherent *here*.
