Overview · sixty-six books

The whole story

Sixty-six books, one long story: God makes a world, a people go wrong and are called, judged, and kept, and in the end the story opens onto a city where there is no more curse. This page is the map. Each book has a proper summary, then a chapter guide — one line per chapter — so you can see the shape before you open the text.

Old Testament

The Old Testament is Israel’s scriptures: law, history, songs, and prophets. It runs from creation to a people waiting, after exile, for God to act again.

Law

The Torah — five books. How the world begins, how a family becomes a nation, and the covenant given at Sinai.

Beginnings

Genesis

50 chapters · 35,639 words

Genesis is the Bible’s first word on almost everything: God, the world, human beings, sin, promise, family, and land. It does not argue that God exists. It begins with God speaking, and a world answering. The first book of the Torah is not a preface you can skip. Everything later — exodus, kingship, exile, gospel — is already in seed here. In the Protestant canon it opens the Law, the five books traditionally tied to Moses, because Israel’s story does not start at the sea: it starts with a world, a fracture, and a family.

The opening chapters are not a modern lab report. They are a story of order out of chaos, of image-bearers who are given a garden and a limit, and of the fracture that follows when the limit is treated as optional. Adam and Eve eat; Cain kills Abel; the flood drowns a violent world; Noah’s family starts again and still gets drunk; a tower at Babel shows how far the fracture runs. Then God does something smaller and stranger: he calls one man, Abram, and ties the healing of the nations to a family.

Out of slavery

Exodus

40 chapters · 30,568 words

Exodus is the story of a God who hears slaves, and the second book of the Torah because Genesis ended in a coffin. A family in Egypt has become a people. The question is no longer whose son is Joseph, but whether the God of Abraham still remembers a cry. In the Protestant canon this is the book of rescue and of making: out of the sea, then into a covenant, then into a tent. Passover, law, and dwelling all start here.

A new king in Egypt has forgotten Joseph. Israel multiplies, is oppressed, and cries out. Hebrew boys are thrown into the Nile; one of them, Moses, is drawn out of it and raised in Pharaoh’s house. He kills an Egyptian, flees to Midian, marries Zipporah, and keeps sheep until God names himself from a bush that burns and is not consumed: I AM WHO I AM, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Moses is sent back with a demand that sounds simple and is not: let my people go, that they may serve me.

Holy, in the camp

Leviticus

27 chapters · 23,384 words

Leviticus is what holiness looks like when God lives next door. After Exodus puts the tent up, the question is not ‘is God with us?’ but ‘how do we live if he is?’ It sits third in the Torah because the story has moved from rescue to nearness. Offerings, priests, clean and unclean, blood, and a day when the nation’s sin is carried away — this is not primitive hygiene. It is a grammar of nearness. In the Protestant canon it is easy to skip. The first hearers could not: this was how a camp stayed a people with a Holy One in the middle.

The first movement is the sanctuary. Burnt offering, grain, peace, sin, guilt — animals, flour, fat, and blood, with rules about who may eat what remains. Aaron and his sons are consecrated: washing, vestments, anointing oil, seven days at the door of the tent. Fire comes out from before the Lord and consumes the offering; the people shout and fall on their faces. That is the first major turn: the system works. God accepts the priested gift. Nearness is possible.

Wilderness

Numbers

36 chapters · 30,837 words

Numbers is the wilderness book of the Torah, named in English for its two censuses, and in Hebrew for its setting ‘in the wilderness’. It sits after Leviticus because the people now have a law and a tent, and the next question is whether they will go up into the land. It should have been a short march from Sinai to Canaan. It becomes forty years because the people refuse the land when they see the giants, and God refuses to pretend that refusal is faith. In the Protestant canon it is the long middle of the Law: formation that failed, then a second generation numbered for a war their parents would not fight.

It opens with a census of fighting men and a camp arranged around the tent: Judah to the east, the Levites inward, trumpets, banners, the cloud. Offerings at the dedication, Nazirites, the Aaronic blessing. Then the first turn: complaint. Taberah, the grave of greed, quail until it comes out of their nostrils. Miriam and Aaron speak against Moses because of the Cushite wife; Miriam is shut out with leprosy for seven days. Twelve spies go up from the wilderness of Paran. Ten come back talking of Anakim and grasshoppers. Caleb and Joshua cannot carry the vote. The people choose a captain to go back to Egypt.

Moses’ last word

Deuteronomy

34 chapters · 26,812 words

Deuteronomy is Moses’ last long day. On the far side of the Jordan, in the land of Moab, he speaks to people who were children at Sinai. The title means ‘second law’: not a photocopy of Exodus, but the law preached again before the crossing. It sits last in the Torah because the wilderness is over and the land is not yet taken. In the Protestant canon it is the hinge between the five books of Moses and the history that begins with Joshua. Later, Josiah’s reformers will find a book in the temple that sounds like this one.

The first movement is memory. Moses retells the journey from Horeb: spies, refusal, wandering, Sihon and Og, the land already taken east of the Jordan. He appoints cities of refuge on that side. Then the heart of the book: Hear, O Israel: the Lord is one. Love him with all your heart, soul, and strength. These words on hand and doorpost and the teaching of children. The first major turn is that the law is not museum furniture. It is a way of life in a land they have not yet entered, pressed on a generation that did not stand at the mountain as adults.

History

From the land, through kings and a split nation, into exile and a fragile return. What happens when the people try (and fail) to live the covenant.

Into the land

Joshua

24 chapters · 17,626 words

Joshua is the crossing. Moses is dead; the promise is not. In the Protestant canon the Former Prophets begin here: not more Torah, but the history of how the people took — and failed to finish taking — the land sworn to Abraham. The book is named for Moses’ successor, the spy who had already said the land could be taken. Be strong and courageous is not a motivational poster. It is an order to a man who has to take a people into a country that already has walls, kings, and altars.

Two spies go into Jericho and are hidden by Rahab, who ties a scarlet cord in her window. The Jordan is in flood; the ark goes first; the waters stand; twelve stones are taken from the riverbed. At Gilgal the new generation is circumcised; the manna stops; they eat the produce of Canaan. A man with a drawn sword: commander of the Lord’s army. Jericho falls in a way that gives God the credit — six days of silence, a seventh of shouting — and Rahab’s household is spared. The first major turn is that the gift has begun. The second is immediate: Achan takes devoted things, and Ai does not fall.

Return and rebuild

Ezra

10 chapters · 7,060 words

Ezra is return. In the Protestant canon it follows Chronicles so closely that the last sentences of 2 Chronicles and the first of Ezra overlap: Cyrus opens the way. The book is named for the scribe who arrives only in chapter 7; the first half is a generation earlier. It sits among the history books as the Bible’s picture of coming home when home is a ruin — not conquest, not monarchy, but a people under empire learning to be Israel again without a king of their own.

Cyrus king of Persia issues the decree. A first wave comes with Sheshbazzar and then Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel and Jeshua the priest, bearing temple vessels that Nebuchadnezzar had taken. They rebuild the altar on its base and keep the Feast of Booths. In the second year they lay the foundation of the house; priests in vestments, cymbals, and a shout. Old men who had seen the former house weep while others shout for joy, and the sound cannot be told apart. That is the first major turn: the work is holy, and it is already smaller than memory.

The wall, and the book

Nehemiah

13 chapters · 9,990 words

Nehemiah is a cupbearer in Persia who weeps over Jerusalem’s broken wall and then asks for leave, letters, and timber. In the Protestant canon it is the companion of Ezra — often one book in Hebrew — and the last of the history sequence before Esther’s court tale. The temple stands; the city is still exposed. The book is named for a layman, not a priest: leadership as prayer, organisation, and conflict, in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes.

Hanani and men from Judah bring the news: the wall is broken down and the gates are burned. Nehemiah weeps, fasts, and prays the covenant story — Abraham, the exodus, the warning in Moses that scattering would follow unfaithfulness, and the promise of gathering. He asks the king; the queen is sitting beside him. Letters to governors, timber from Asaph keeper of the king’s forest, an escort. He rides the circuit at night, tells the priests and nobles, and sets the people to build by families, gate by gate. Sanballat the Horonite, Tobiah the Ammonite, and Geshem the Arabian mock. The first turn is that the work begins anyway.

Esther

10 chapters · 5,413 words

Esther is set in the Persian court, among Jews who did not go home. In the Protestant canon it sits after Nehemiah as the last of the history books, though it is a different kind of writing: a court tale, a feast book, the origin of Purim. The temple and the wall are offstage. The king is Ahasuerus, lavish and dangerous, ruling from India to Ethiopia. The book is named for a Jewish orphan who becomes queen without advertising who her people are.

A banquet of a hundred and eighty days, then seven more. Vashti refuses to be displayed and is dismissed by a royal edict about husbands ruling their houses. Young women are gathered. Mordecai sits at the gate, uncovers a plot by Bigthan and Teresh, and is recorded in the chronicles. The first major turn is Haman the Agagite, promoted, slighted because Mordecai will not bow, deciding to destroy every Jew in the empire and casting lots — pur — for the day.

Suffering, and no tidy answer

Job

42 chapters · 17,649 words

Job is the Bible’s longest argument about innocent suffering, and it sits among the Wisdom books because it tests wisdom where the sayings fail. A blameless man in the land of Uz loses children, wealth, and health in a heavenly wager he never hears. The Accuser asks whether Job fears God for nothing. Permission is given, then given again, down to the skin. Proverbs will tell a young person how the world often works. Job is what happens when a good life is stripped and the old equations do not hold. The book is not a puzzle with a moral on the last page. It is a permitted quarrel.

The opening is prose, almost a folk tale, until it is not. Messengers arrive one after another: oxen, sheep, camels, and then the house where the young people were eating. Job tears his robe, shaves his head, and worships: the Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away. Sores cover him from foot to head. His wife says curse God and die. He will not. Three friends — Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite, Zophar the Naamathite — come to sit seven days in silence, which is the one kind thing they do. Then Job opens his mouth and curses his birth. Why did I not die from the womb? The poetry has begun, and it will not be tidy.

The prayerbook

Psalms

150 chapters · 40,137 words

The Psalms are Israel’s prayerbook: one hundred and fifty songs of praise, complaint, trust, royal hope, wisdom, and raw anger. They sit in Wisdom not because they are calm, but because they teach a people how to speak — to God, about enemies, about the king, about the poor. They were meant to be sung. Five books sit inside the one book, each closing with a doxology, the last a run of hallelujahs, as if the collection itself were a small Torah of the mouth. Doctrine is here, but it arrives as voice, not as a system. A person without words of their own has always been able to borrow these.

The book opens with a gate: two ways, the blessed who delights in the law, the wicked like chaff the wind drives away. Then a royal psalm — why do the nations rage? — and the collection is already mixing instruction, kingship, and the Lord’s rule. David’s name is on many; Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon, Moses, Ethan, Heman, and none at all stand on others. Laments start early and do not apologise. How long, O Lord? My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Enemies are named. The poor are heard. Kings are addressed as if their justice matters to heaven. The first movement is already the whole art: bless, protest, trust, and refuse to pretend.

Habakkuk is a prophet who argues, and the book is a dialogue, not a speech from a pulpit. It sits among the Prophets because the word of the Lord answers a man’s complaint about violence in Judah — and then uses a worse violence as the answer. O Lord, how long shall I cry for help, and you will not hear? Or cry to you ‘Violence!’ and you will not save? The law is paralysed. Justice never goes forth. The wicked surround the righteous. God’s first answer is worse: look among the nations, and see; I am raising up the Chaldeans, that bitter and hasty nation, who march through the earth to take possession of dwelling places not their own. They laugh at every fortress.

Habakkuk objects again. Are you not from everlasting, O Lord my God, my Holy One? We shall not die. You who are of purer eyes than to see evil, and cannot look at wrong, why do you look at the treacherous, and are silent when the wicked swallows up the man more righteous than he? Human beings like fish of the sea, caught with a hook, dragged in a net. Shall he then keep on emptying his net and mercilessly killing nations forever? The second complaint is theodicy with the empire already on the road.

Mark

It belongs as a line drawn at the door.

Hospitality, and a name

3 John

1 chapter · 293 words

3 John is to Gaius, whom the elder loves in truth. It is a private note about hospitality, authority, and a man who wants to be first. The Protestant canon keeps it as a small window on how the early churches treated travelling workers — and how a man who wants control can shut a door the gospel wanted open.

The elder to the beloved Gaius. I pray you may prosper in all things and be in health, as it is well with your soul. Fellow workers have testified to his hospitality, walking in the truth. You do well to send them on their journey in a manner worthy of God, for they have gone out for the name, taking nothing from the Gentiles. The opening is thanks for an open house.

Contend for the faith

Jude

1 chapter · 604 words

Jude meant to write about their common salvation, and instead found he had to contend for the faith once for all delivered to the saints. It is a short letter in the name of Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James. The Protestant canon keeps it last before the unveiling. It belongs because grace can be perverted into sensuality, and because the church needed a warning that was also a doxology.

Beloved, certain people have crept in unnoticed, ungodly people who pervert the grace of our God into sensuality and deny our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ. Mercy, peace, and love were the greeting; contention is the job.

The Lamb, and the city

Revelation

22 chapters · 11,385 words

Revelation is an unveiling, not a puzzle book. It is the last book of the Protestant canon: a prophecy in letters and visions, given to John on Patmos for seven churches in Asia, and kept by the church because the story of Scripture cannot end with a prison or a rumour. It belongs because it shows who actually rules when beasts look like they do, and because its last word is not a beast. It is a city, and a welcome.

John is on Patmos. He sees one like a son of man among seven lampstands, and writes to seven churches — Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia, Laodicea — commendation, warning, a promise to the one who conquers. First love lost, tribulation, a white stone, Jezebel, a name that lives and is dead, an open door, lukewarm water. Then a door in heaven, a throne, holy, holy, holy, and a slain Lamb who is worthy to open a scroll. The book begins on an island and in a throne room at once.
