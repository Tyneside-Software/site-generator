The Psalms are Israel’s prayerbook: one hundred and fifty songs of praise, complaint, trust, royal hope, wisdom, and raw anger. They sit in Wisdom not because they are calm, but because they teach a people how to speak — to God, about enemies, about the king, about the poor. They were meant to be sung. Five books sit inside the one book, each closing with a doxology, the last a run of hallelujahs, as if the collection itself were a small Torah of the mouth. Doctrine is here, but it arrives as voice, not as a system. A person without words of their own has always been able to borrow these.

The book opens with a gate: two ways, the blessed who delights in the law, the wicked like chaff the wind drives away. Then a royal psalm — why do the nations rage? — and the collection is already mixing instruction, kingship, and the Lord’s rule. David’s name is on many; Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon, Moses, Ethan, Heman, and none at all stand on others. Laments start early and do not apologise. How long, O Lord? My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Enemies are named. The poor are heard. Kings are addressed as if their justice matters to heaven. The first movement is already the whole art: bless, protest, trust, and refuse to pretend.

The middle of the Psalter is not a plot. It is a library. There are laments that refuse to end in a bow, hymns that do, songs of ascent for the road to Zion, royal psalms for a son of David, and a long love-letter to the law (Psalm 119). Psalm 22 moves from forsakenness to the ends of the earth remembering. Psalm 23 pastures a table in the presence of enemies. Psalm 51 is a broken spirit after a king’s crime. Psalm 73 nearly slips seeing the wicked prosper, then enters the sanctuary. The imprecatory psalms ask God to break teeth and drown the enemy’s children. They are in the book on purpose. So is ‘the Lord is my light and my salvation,’ and so is the pilgrim’s ‘I was glad when they said to me, let us go to the house of the Lord.’

It ends as it must: let everything that has breath praise the Lord. The last five psalms are almost nothing but praise, as if the complaints have been gathered in and the collection will still not close on a curse. What is left hanging is the king. The royal psalms promise a son, a throne, nations as inheritance, and history after David is a long wait. Zion is still both gift and wound. The enemies are not all gone. The poor are still in the text, still heard. A people can finish the book singing and still be in exile, still under empire, still waiting for the horn of David to be raised.

Read them as prayers first, not as a doctrine outline. The hard parts are the violence asked for, the silence of God in the long how-longs, and the royal claims that sit uneasily after the throne is gone. Christians have sung these as Christ’s prayers as well as David’s — Psalm 22 at the cross, the stone the builders rejected, a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek. Jews have sung them through exile and return, morning and evening. Either way the book is a school of attention: how to rejoice, how to wait, how to accuse God without leaving. Do not skip the psalms that will not tidy themselves for church. They are there so that piety cannot forbid the truth in the mouth.
