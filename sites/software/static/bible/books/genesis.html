Genesis is the Bible’s first word on almost everything: God, the world, human beings, sin, promise, family, and land. It does not argue that God exists. It begins with God speaking, and a world answering. The first book of the Torah is not a preface you can skip. Everything later — exodus, kingship, exile, gospel — is already in seed here. In the Protestant canon it opens the Law, the five books traditionally tied to Moses, because Israel’s story does not start at the sea: it starts with a world, a fracture, and a family.

The opening chapters are not a modern lab report. They are a story of order out of chaos, of image-bearers who are given a garden and a limit, and of the fracture that follows when the limit is treated as optional. Adam and Eve eat; Cain kills Abel; the flood drowns a violent world; Noah’s family starts again and still gets drunk; a tower at Babel shows how far the fracture runs. Then God does something smaller and stranger: he calls one man, Abram, and ties the healing of the nations to a family.

From Abraham the line runs through Sarah’s barrenness and Isaac nearly lost on Moriah, through Jacob — renamed Israel — who steals a blessing and wrestles at the Jabbok, and through twelve sons. The book is honest about them. There is deceit, favouritism, Dinah and Shechem, Judah and Tamar, and a coat that starts a famine-era rescue. Joseph is sold, rises in Egypt under Pharaoh, and feeds the family that wronged him. The household goes down to Goshen as guests, not yet as slaves.

Genesis ends not in the promised land but in a coffin in Egypt, waiting. Jacob has blessed Ephraim and Manasseh, spoken over the twelve, claimed burial at Machpelah, and died. Joseph makes his brothers swear that his bones will go up when God visits them. The promise is still in force. The people are not home yet. A clan has become the seed of a nation, parked beside the Nile, with a famine behind them and a new king — unnamed here — still unwritten.

Read Genesis slowly enough to notice the repetitions: two creation tellings, paired dreams, blessings given in the wrong order. Names matter — Eve, Israel, Bethel, Peniel — because the story is making a people who will remember who named them. God here walks, bargains with Abraham over Sodom, and wrestles in the dark. He is not an idea. The violence and the family mess are the soil the promise has to grow in. Exodus is next: a new king who did not know Joseph, and a cry Genesis has already taught you God can hear.
